CHAPTER I

HOW TO ATTRACT THE MAN YOU DESIRE


Now what, really, _is_ love? We are here talking of love between the
sexes--that is, the state of being "in love"--not of love between
parents and children and friends, and brother to brother, sister to
sister, or love for humanity. Love between the sexes, taken in the
guise in which it shows itself to us on this planet, is the spiritual
and glamorous disguise which envelops the coarser nature instinct for
re-creation of species. A man or a woman in love is unconsciously drawn
by the mating instinct--no matter how he or she may truly believe that
the desire for union and nearness and companionship is something purely
spiritual. Nature's message is always carry on--reproduce--propagate.
And so, since we live on the earth planet, we must all be affected
by Nature's laws. The lowest animal stage of life obeys this urge,
but one of the things which differentiates man from the animal is his
capacity to amalgamate the divine spiritual emotion with the instinct
for re-creation. The spiritual part of love, in the broad sense, is
sacrifice--that is, all individuals, while in love, desire to curb
selfish desires in the interest of the loved one, however selfish the
natural character may be. If this sacrificial element is not in the
emotion, then it is not really love, but merely a demonstration of
animal passion.

The less there is of the spiritual element in the state of being in
love, the shorter will be the passion's duration because of the nature
law of decay. Spiritual things are divine and eternal, but all that
is of the earth, is subject to decay and change. An animal passion
destroys itself with satiation--and since it is animal, it is only
mated upon the physical plane and goes the way of all physical things.

Love between the sexes is the sole real happiness--everything else is
second best--a very good makeshift, sometimes, but mating with the
loved one is the only complete joy on this earth.

Then how to achieve this joy? Before she marries, a girl practically
has her life in her own hands--afterwards she has taken on either
some limitation or some responsibility. As a general principle, not
to show too much eagerness is the first thing to remember, the object
being to secure your heart's desire, for even if he is the meekest
male creature, a man likes to think that he can do his own hunting! In
this country where every girl can have two or three swains to choose
from, she can give herself more airs than in any other, the men from
their cradles having been accustomed to the dominating influence of
women--they are trained to take their lead from them, and generally
do. Women's ways with men are quite different to men's ways with
women--because through all the ages, women have had to secure their
desires by intelligence as opposed to force. So this has developed in
them an instinct which does not come from reason. They act from impulse
often--they have intuition where men have no guide. And so, even at
this hour of freedom for them, the old methods still show in their
treatment of men and situations. They use deliberate cunning and are
more or less natural vampires. So, being endowed with this heritage,
a girl has only to want any particular man and she can generally get
him. I am now going to cite cases where the initial desire has been on
the woman's side and the man is not yet illuminated. The clever girl
begins by watching the man's character, likes, and dislikes. Then she
stimulates his interest in whatever subject she has discovered holds
his thoughts. He finds himself drawn by her sympathy. Or it may be
wiser in some cases for her to present a picture to him of a woman the
exact opposite to what he believes that he likes, and the surprise
arouses his interest--for the main thing is to attract his attention
and arouse his interest. The girl is too intelligent to talk about
herself and her likes and dislikes, or to show egotism in any way. A
man not yet interested in a woman becomes bored to death directly she
speaks of herself, although, when he grows to be in love with her, this
is one of the subjects which interests him most! The girl out on the
hunt has to study the methods of hunters--their quarry is approached
according to the nature of the beast. A man would never expect to
secure a timid deer by running at it and shouting--nor would he use
the same methods in hunting tigers that he would employ in ensnaring
rabbits. When men go a-hunting, they really put whatever intelligence
they have into the game. But thousands of women, hunting that rare and
complicated animal, man, just express themselves and then blame fate
for not giving them the mates they wanted. But if you control your ego
and study the man--let him understand that he will probably receive
sympathy for his fads and tastes with you--or in the other case that
his curiosity and fighting instincts will have stimulation--you will
have aroused his interest. Then it lies in your own hands, girls, how
deeply you ensnare the poor fellow--as once his interest is aroused
you have only to feed it and he is yours. Of course there are natures
which may be utterly antagonistic, and nothing you could do or say
could draw them to you. When you have become convinced of this, then
have courage to control your own emotion and give up the quest. There
is nothing so pitifully contemptible as a woman in love with a man
who does not care for her. So when they first meet men, I would warn
young girls to try and hold back every feeling which may be aroused.
Never give easily. Do not give at all until you know that the man is
attracted really, and then only in small doses--for, in front of every
woman are always the three specters, of Satiety--Man's Polygamous
Nature--and his Hunting Instinct. It is unnatural for him to be
faithful to you--he only can remain so if you fulfill his need for
variety--and in yourself supply the answer to all his vagrant wants.
Never bore him or satiate him--or let him slumber in peaceful certainty
about you!--or a force far beyond his control will tempt him to look
elsewhere.

It is much more easy to make a man in love with you than to keep him
so. Tremendously popular girls often show ten beaux languishing for
them one season and quite indifferent to them the next. And then some
quiet, unimportant looking creature will arouse the passionate love
of one or two worth while men and hold them for years. To analyze
the reason of this, it is because the popular girl is dissipating
her forces. She is so accustomed to be surrounded by men and receive
their admiration that she takes no pains to please any of them, but
just expresses HERSELF--and "herself" being interesting on the surface
because of "sex appeal"--(that dreadful word!)--she holds the men's
desires until they have realized that there is nothing to respect--much
in her character--or really anything mysterious to whet their
appetites--and so their infatuation is cured. The quiet, intelligent
maiden has not so much vanity--and realized early that the buzzing
of suitors is no real compliment, so she concentrated upon the few
who were worthy of notice, studied them--and kept them chained to her
chariot wheels.

As a general principle, to attract men, girls must be versatile--able
to talk--able to dance and play games--able to be "pals" and able to
be illusive mysteries. They must appear natural even if they are not in
reality, and above all, they must be clever enough never to wound mens'
vanity. Once a girl has done this, a bitter sense of resentment is
aroused which produces antagonism and the game is up. Do not accept too
much from an admirer--be chary of dining or dancing with him whenever
he asks you to. Never give him the impression that he is the sole
entertainer you know.




CHAPTER II

THE RESULTS OF PETTING AND DRINKING


Everywhere you go nowadays you confront petting. Not, perhaps, that you
see people actually fondling, but you read about it in the newspapers,
hear it denounced from the pulpit, listen to it discussed on all sides.

There always has been and always will be petting. It is natural for
man, after a brief or extended period of courtship, to demonstrate his
fondness for his lady in some fashion. I do not remember that I ever
have heard anyone deride such a logical condition between enamored
sexes. The great discussion seems to center about the boy and the
girl who, heretofore utter strangers, upon their very first meeting
straightway proceed to get down to the business of raining incessant
kisses and caresses upon each other, then promptly proceed to forget.

I do not think that petting is quite so dangerous as some would have
us believe, at least not so far as actual indiscretion is concerned.
Petting is just a habit, the hallmark of sophistication, the badge
of a certain smartness, the proof of an absolute divorce from the
so-called foolish staidness of tiresome older folks. I have always
advocated keeping up with the times, yet I do not feel that this
incessant indulgence in promiscuous mauling achieves anything of value.
I have observed that, the more petting goes on, the less it means to
the individual. After a hundred petting parties, the process becomes
automatic--the boy is no more thrilled by her kisses than he is by
those of his mother, while the girl could, with her eyes shut, derive
as much pleasure from embracing a wooden Indian.

So that petting burns itself out in time. Most girls get through safely
to the stage of satiety. Others, by reason of their more passionate
natures, do not. An intensely warm and affectionate nature would
do well to avoid a too hasty and promiscuous indulgence in these
questionable joys. While most men do not use petting to an end, a
girl's too warm and ready response might suddenly awaken him to the
fact that there might be certain possibilities.

So many of our young women of to-day seem to have achieved the
impression that "slow" or shy girls are unpopular and are shunned by
men who do not wish to go to the trouble of manœuvering a backward one
up to the point of petting. Such is not the case. The man is interested
in the chase, but his enthusiasm quickly dies when the quarry is safely
in his gamebag. When the chase is extremely easy, the hunter may not
even bother to pick his game up, but may leave it right where he caught
it. While, on the other hand, if the hunt has been unusually hot and
thrilling, if the game has been wary and tireless, the man, when he has
caught it, goes about proudly exhibiting his latest catch and asking
everyone if it is not an exceptionally fine bird.

So, girls, when you are parading your conquests before the eyes of
the world and think you are the envy of so many others, think back
carefully over all these affairs. Ask yourself the length of time these
numerous suitors, after storming your heart with their endless kisses,
have held on to prove their affection and their smooth avowals of love.
Certainly, young one, you must wish to be treated with respect--you do
not want young men to think you are easy, or cheap, or common--just one
act in a many-ringed circus. Most assuredly you do not. But do these
irrepressible young men always treat you with the courtesy and decency
that woman must demand, first and all, from any man? Or, when the boy
attempts to tell questionable stories, or to take undue liberties with
you, do you say to yourself, "Oh, dear! They are all alike--one must
expect it of them and try to overlook such things." No woman ever
cheapened herself or lost one iota of popularity through demanding the
respect of any man.

"But," you say, "it is so much fun to go around and meet all the nice
boys and have a good time. These convent girls don't seem to enjoy
themselves at all." Foolish, very, very foolish. No fun-loving girl
need to conduct herself severely or primly to ward off undesirable
advances. The most loved and lovable girls are the jolly, healthy,
sensible ones who know that the less men see of them, the more they
are sought after. These young women find men delightful companions in
sports and at dances, but it is not essential to their happiness that
they be immediately entwined in the arms of each and every stranger
that wanders into their ken.

Some communities, I understand, have laws against automobile petting
parties. It is true enough that some of the main highways and many of
the byroads are heavily picketed at night by cars of all makes--lights
out, curtains drawn. But, it appears to me, the attempt to make
unlawful this process of spooning might be interpreted as a direct blow
at personal liberties. The young people cannot be supposed to remain
home on all these beautiful summer evenings; certainly they cannot
stroll safely hand in hand along our state roads with cars thundering
by and lights blinding and revealing them.

In some respects, the automobile has become a disturbing element in
the lives of boys and girls. In years past, courtship progressed at
the girl's home, on lovers'-lane strolls, at parties, dances, and the
like, while to-day the automobile and good roads enable the young
people quickly to reach comparatively distant points of entertainment.
Roadhouses and wayside inns have sprung up at places of necessary and
desirable remoteness, where the restraints of nearby residents are not
available for quelling unruly emotions and passions that follow coarse
music and inferior liquor. So, young girls, unless you have been reared
with a happy sense of restraint and the fitness of things, you endanger
your peace of mind and your good repute by frequenting roadhouses in
the company of casual male companions.

True it is that parents are much to blame for a certain slackness in
the training and observation of their children. Father and mother,
with their car, cannot always expect daughter and her friend to be
satisfied to motor quietly about with them. If the parents are too
often joy-riding alone themselves, they can hardly expect daughter to
remain at home looking over the latest styles or trying out the newest
fox-trot.

And, young girls, if you must joy-ride, insist that your escort return
you home at an early hour. Do not frequent roadhouses where they serve
liquor. Even though you may not drink yourself, you will be credited
with having done so. In these days of abominable bootleg poison and lax
standards, you may regret to the end of your days the moment you began
drinking. Liquor is not for the young. Your blood is fresh and vigorous
enough to give you all the stimulus you need. You are naturally
vivacious. Impressions are new. You can have simply wonderful times
without the assistance of liquor. Spirits will deaden your sense, will
lull to sleep your caution, will lead you gradually and unknowingly and
pleasantly to indiscretions that your later awakening will horribly
contemplate. Heed not the taunts and sneers of your male escort or any
other of the ladies of the party who may be indulging in liquor--the
men may wish you to drink for a purpose, while it cuts the pride of
the other girls to have you refrain. Such companions are not your
friends--they will injure you, not help you.

Be you a girl of eighteen or a woman of forty, a too great indulgence
in liquor on any given occasion is not only shocking taste, but will
disgust any man more quickly than any other ill-favored action. Even
the slightly intoxicated woman has utterly lost her sweetness, her
womanliness, her charm. Men, avoid as you would the plague, all women
and girls who are too frequent sippers at the cup. They are sure to
humiliate, disgrace, or disgust you.

The trouble with you men, though, is that you jokingly condone such
vulgar actions on the part of these foolish young flappers who seem to
be so badly trained in a sense of values by those in charge of their
youth, that by the time they reach twenty-five they will have lost all
capacity for happiness. I remember a petting party I saw in a certain
picture not long ago in which a girl became so intoxicated that she had
to be put under the shower in the bath-room and then lost her clothes
and had to go home in part of a bathing suit--too drunk to know or care!

Perhaps some of my readers may have seen such scenes in real life. I
am thankful to say that I have not, but the really hideous part is not
so much in the girl being disgustingly drunk, but in the attitude of
mind about it, of all concerned. No one was in the least revolted, all
thought it a huge joke, and it was obvious that no stigma would attach
itself to the girl next day!

So if this is the opinion of the majority, the outlook is rather
hopeless--and that it must be the opinion of the majority is evident
from the enchanted laughter of the audience and the fact that there
would not be so many moving pictures about the subject if it were not
real. Nor would I receive dozens of letters asking me if this is the
right view to take, namely, one of tolerance and amused indifference to
the license of the day.

It is impossible to stand against the spirit of a time. And if this
cheapening of all things which another generation hallowed is an
expression of the convictions of this age, then it will have to be
accepted, and there is no use in elderly people being unhappy about
it. They must look upon it as an epidemic which has grown beyond their
control and has to just work itself out, killing whom it will, in its
path to extinction.

It is possible to stop an epidemic in its first stages, but not when
it has spread through a whole country. And the sensible thing to do
about it is to turn attention from those affected with it to the young
children who are still at an age when their subconscious minds can be
trained in any way which pleases the parents. What is happening now is
initially the fault of the parents, and what will happen in the future
will be up to them also. And that is all which can be said about the
matter.

Though it is sad that the lovely, much-pawed, tipsy girls cannot
realize that they are "worse than wicked--they are vulgar!"

Try to realize, my young friends, that this petting, this drinking,
this wild, empty, vulgar life is rapidly robbing you of your youth--and
what is more precious than Youth? Precious Youth, which no one seems
to value until it is fading. I am longing all the time to help young
people to keep it and not squander it as fast as they can. That is my
attitude, not an unsympathetic one of censure, or a belief that all old
things are necessarily better than new ones.

This generation has several good qualities, more accentuated than
those of the last one; it has more courage, more independence, more
self-reliance, and more capability to fend for itself, to secure a
living in the material sense. But what it has not achieved is a sense
of values.

So the young people are busy throwing away the vital forces of youth
without getting any real return, except the most momentary and ordinary
pleasure. And they are laying up for themselves heavy limitations for
the long thirties, forties, and fifties which will come to them, if
they live. For the human machine will stand only a certain amount of
poison being poured into it, without showing it outwardly. When once
the limit is passed, then every sort of ugly thing appears for all to
see. The beautiful fairy-like blonde of eighteen, who swallows down
two or three cocktails before dinner, and as much whiskey or gin as
she can get afterwards, never thinks these things are going to spoil
her complexion in a year or two, or that the cigarettes she smokes can
contain any drug which will irritate nerves or weaken the will.

She takes them all as part of her day's pleasure--which all her
friends share. And for a long time she will not notice the very faint
change which her mirror could show her--the line of cheek not quite so
perfect, the flesh generally not so firm, and the capacity for pleasure
not so great, while the craving for excitement grows more insistent.

If these lovely young people were all going to be swallowed up by
an earthquake when they reach the age of thirty, then I would say,
if spiritual things do not matter to them, cram as much emotion as
possible into the short time, and never look ahead. But with that long,
long period until sixty or seventy to pass, in all probability, it does
seem unbusiness-like to act in a way for twelve years which will make
the thirty or forty afterwards almost unbearable. That is not having
horse-sense!

I have been coming backwards and forwards to this country now for four
years, and each time I arrive again, alas! I see some slight change
in numbers of young faces--just a little more haggard, just a little
less spiritual, just a little harder. And always the ones who show this
alteration are those who drink and smoke to excess, while the ones who
are rising in fame and beauty and fortune have moderation for their
motive.

Drink and get drunk--smoke and get nervous--if you want to, after
realizing what these things will bring you. You are, after all, free
citizens. But for heaven's sake, don't just drift on, imagining that
miracles are going to happen to your particular body! And that you will
not have to pay any price!

I have warned you so far against certain dangers that are bound to
overtake you should you indulge too unwisely in petting and drinking.
But the greatest loss of all will be the gradual draining of your
capacity to love, to know love when it comes to you. The white fever
pitch of love cannot go on forever. The emotions are capable of only
so much--the nerves are not always going to respond to kisses with the
original thrill. You hear and read much about the joy of the first
kiss. Now, will these petting fiends, when the kisses reach up into the
thousands, gather the thrill and romance from such signs of affection
as they received from the first? Hardly. And yet they seem perfectly
willing to go on frittering away their capacity to love to the first
comer.

Perhaps you may think I am too severe with the thoughtless young. But
love is the greatest force that God has given us. We must guard and
cherish it, for this heavenly emotion is not like a clock we can wind
up each day, year after year. In my opinion, it is partially the fault
of the women that they have let down the bars. The Spanish custom of
watching carefully each step of engaged couples is founded on excellent
psychology. The suitor chaffs under the restraint. What he wants now
he must wait for. The caresses and kisses his heart calls for are
given very meagerly, if at all. He thus comes to the marriage altar
with none of his desires satisfied, his imagination running wild. It
almost appears that the young man of to-day has nothing to imagine. Too
often he has indulged in so many of the pleasures and ecstasies of the
married state.

Man wishes most to acquire what he cannot have, or seems to be unable
to secure. He wants what the other men are striving so feverishly to
own. He is not interested in what he can have for the mere asking. Come
easy, go easy. What he works hard for he will appreciate.

There is a growing tendency for flappers to romance with married
men. Most of our sensible girls' instincts will tell them that the
attentions of a married man are merely an insult, disguised in whatever
form they happen to be. Usually, it is only the lowest and cheapest
sort of girl who willingly encourages such people, blazoning to the
world that her vanity is colossal and her self-respect nil. If a man is
not free to marry a girl, his assiduous attentions are an impertinence,
to say the least. Why bother with anything in which you can hope to
gain nothing but disgrace--and, if you are discovered, loss of your
self-respect, loss of everything?

Do not think if you are found out, that you will be accused of anything
but the very worst. Remember, the married man can probably find little
pleasure in your mind. An older, experienced woman might hope to hold
his affections for a certain period, but of the young flapper he soon
tires. It is too much trouble for him. The game is too nerve-wracking;
things are likely to happen unpleasantly at any moment. He is looking
for a little fun. Because he is acquainted with the arts of love and
with the flattering little attentions that mean so much to women, it
will be difficult for the foolish little flapper to withstand his arts
and blandishments. And, if she is there to give, he can hardly be
expected to refuse.

Some women have affairs with married men, knowing beforehand what they
may expect, and do so willingly. With this type I am not interested.
They go into the game with their eyes open, so there is no more to be
said. But my heart bleeds for the blind little thing who tumbles in out
of a spirit of bravado--to be smart--to see what it is like. One girl
will say to another, "Oh, I go out with married men. They are so much
more polite and smooth--and they do not talk." But that is the most
that can be said for the relation. The newspapers are full daily of
the tragedies of ignorant girls who have fallen in love with married
men, realizing only too late that they can expect nothing in return.

The pernicious habit of petting seems, I am informed, to be as largely
practiced by married couples as it is by the younger set. Of course, in
certain gay circles, there always has existed a sort of tacit agreement
that uninterested wives and bored husbands might look about for more
entertaining and thrilling material. Such married couples have lost
most of their mutual love for the reason that their time has been too
greatly occupied with having a good time and with rushing here and
there. There have been no children to subdue them. They have had no
necessity to sacrifice for one another. It has not even been necessary
for them to try to please and amuse each other, because there have been
so many others around who were willing to amuse them.

But I want to speak here of the average married couples who gather at
parties and homes, there too freely to drink and cavort and pet with
other husbands and wives, sometimes even with little tots listening in
or looking on. The average married couple has not the means to indulge
in sports, as do the wealthy, that will help to wash away the tired
eyes and the nasty taste in the mouth, or even the unpleasant memory
of what happened the evening before. There is no riding, no tennis,
no surf-bathing, no other healthful exercises that will bring abused
bodies back to normal. No, John must go to the office or the store,
aching in body, half disgusted in spirit, while Ann rises to a day of
nervous irritations.

Naturally, we must allow married people more freedom of speech and
action than is ordinarily given the unmarried. But no one can expect
the young boys and girls utterly to banish pleasures which they know
their married friends indulge in so flagrantly. Unmarried couples, I
am told, have been frequently invited to such affairs. What a satire
for the young must be these spectacles of happy married life, in which
the husband promptly banishes his wife to another's arms and himself
spends the rest of the evening drinking rotten whiskey and chasing
about after cheap thrills. How happy and carefree they remain, as long
as the liquor flows freely and the music runs wild.

But the next day--the husband looks at Ann. What are his thoughts? Is
he wondering how far she went? Is she resolving impetuously that she
will allow a certain woman's husband to pay more attention to her next
time--that what is good enough for John is good enough for her? And
suspicions arise--discord. Love and romance are dying, fast--the very
best things in life are disappearing, and it is so hard to retrieve
these most precious things after they have once gone. If the soul
becomes thus too hardened, it does not even know what it lacks!

And to you married women who drink freely at the home of some friend,
I would ask you to remember that you are inviting by this drinking the
unwelcome, vulgar intimacies and attentions of men. No man will treat
you any worse or better than you want to be treated, as evidenced by
your character, your manner, and all those other things which go to
make up your personality. Men know, as you know, that the liquor you
drink tends to make you careless in speech and action; they know, as
you know, that while under the influence of alcohol, you do things and
allow liberties that you would instantly repel when in full possession
of your faculties. These men unconsciously argue, then, that if
willingly you allow yourself to get into a condition whereby you may be
approached in disgusting fashion, certainly they will not fail to take
advantage of your foolish laxity.

I have heard single girls bewail the fact that their male companions
did not treat them with proper respect--that some of the reverence
and gentlemanliness with which womanhood should be approached was
entirely lacking--that the men seemed to be getting ruder and coarser.
To such, I merely reply by saying that it is all their own fault--that
men take the hint of their treatment of any woman from the women
themselves--that no man will attempt obnoxious advances of any kind
unless he has hopes they will be encouraged and accepted.

You Americans are extremely proud of your freedom; you will stand
firmly and always on your rights; you will allow not a single one of
them to be trampled on or even threatened. And, if your legislators
pass a law which is disfavored by any great number, then that law will
be ignored openly. Much of the laxity of manners and morals in young
people to-day, for instance, might be ascribed to the fact that the
American legislators told their people they must not drink. I have
nothing to say for or against this law--I want merely to discuss its
results. What are they? Just this: Many liberty-loving persons enjoy
doing most what they are told they cannot do--as if their defiance
were proof positive of absolute sovereignty. And I firmly believe that
tens of thousands of the youth of this country are drinking bootleg
poison, ruining health and minds and morals simply because it appears
romantically and adventurously bad for them to do so. In other years,
when anyone could have it, drunkenness with persons of even average
standing would have been accepted as of extremely poor taste. To-day,
hardly any party, particularly with the young people, seems complete
without liquor. The situation has become a national joke as well as
a national tragedy. The newspapers, the theaters, the magazines, the
movies--all smile at it and seem in utter accord with law infringement.
The lawmakers said: "You shall not drink." And a shout of derision
went up. And drink was glorified. A flask on the hip became a letter
of introduction, the sure card of admittance to any smart, up-to-date
gathering.

Now the question naturally arises: How are the wayward tendencies of
youth to be corrected, controlled, or eradicated? And it seems to
me that the happy solution of this problem depends largely upon the
mothers of America--upon their keeping the respect, confidence, and
pal-ship of their daughters. This ought to be very easy if the right
way is begun in time.

The first thing a mother has to do is to realize what is the spirit of
the age--which certainly is not the same as it was in her generation.
She cannot change it, however much she may dislike it--she had better
analyze what it is and where its tendencies are leading the youth of
the world--and then make up her mind to keep this knowledge always in
mind when she is judging of her own daughters. The spirit of this age
is one of great rebellion against any restraint--a natural resentment
against the law and order. No child will receive commands blindly--each
must know why they are being given. If one home is ruled on strict
lines of other times, its inmates see their young companions all
enjoying freedom, and they either break away immediately or live lives
of deceit.

There are certain fundamental laws, however, of right and wrong, which
go on through all ages and times--and these should be kept and so
instilled into the little girls from birth that by the time they are
twelve years old they will react to them automatically, and if good
taste has been taught at the same time, by twelve the little girl is
really equipped to guide her own life more or less into a harbor of
safety.

But no mother can inspire or keep the confidence of her daughters
if she, herself, acts in a way which they cannot respect. Nor can
she keep their pal-ship when they despise her. Youth is merciless in
its judgments, tolerance only comes later on. So that, to keep these
precious things, the mother must have made her little girls love her
from the beginning. She must have entered with sympathy into all their
tastes, hopes and fears--she must have made herself respected, so that
they feel it is an honor to be called "pally" with her--and yet, that
they are so sure of her love that there is no fear in the relationship.

When the girls seem to be going ahead with modern freedom, which really
shocks the mother in her heart, she must comfort herself by knowing
that if she has brought up their spirits in the right way, they will
not fall beneath their own self-respect, even if their companions do
under the new license. Then the mother must explain what the results
of such and such actions will be--without showing disapproval--just as
one friend to another, she being the friend who perhaps has had the
most experience, and therefore can be helpful. In this way she can
guard and keep her daughters without wounding that weird little upstart
vanity--which all young things have, feeling that they know better
than any elder can possibly do! To pander to every whim, to condone
every lapse, is not to win confidence or pal-ships from children. Love,
sympathy and explanations are the true roads.

If Kitty has been properly trained but goes out on one of these
incredible parties with other college girls and boys--where they
have quantities of whiskey flasks, and nearly all of them become
under the influence of alcohol--she will not go too far for her own
pride's sake, and she will tell her mother all about it--and the
mother will be sympathetic and endeavor to influence her _taste_ not
to wish to over-indulge in these entertainments. And Kitty's love for
her mother will make her not want to wound her. The worst of it is
that such a large percentage of the young generation now are so much
more intelligent than their parents! More "cute"--(I hate that word
but it expresses the modern spirit)--they are armed with superficial
education, futile philosophies and arguments--to an extent which
makes it hopeless for the parents to contend with them. So that I
give this advice in one sentence: Mothers, _make yourselves worthy of
respect_--then give out boundless love, and understanding--and God will
take care of the rest.




CHAPTER III

FRANK ADVICE TO UNMARRIED GIRLS


A clever lady interviewer once came to ask me what I thought about
Summer holiday flirtations; were they right or wrong? It was impossible
to say what I wanted to in an interview, but it struck me as a subject
which is of vital importance to most single girls. I am given to
understand that numbers of girls go off on their two or three weeks'
vacation perhaps with a girl friend, where they meet young men and have
a wonderful time. They become engaged, with all the privileges which
engagement implies--and perhaps they marry--and perhaps they do not.

Ought they to enter into these transient relationships or ought they to
abstain? That was the question asked--and an impossible one to answer
in a general way. Certainly, gray lives of work deserve a week or two
of gaiety and pleasure. And if both parties enter into it with the same
joyous thought of passing a happy time with no aftermath of sorrow
attached to it, there cannot be any harm done. But, unfortunately,
one or the other may grow to care deeply--and what then? Or one or
the other might be of a very passionate nature and the temptation
of unlimited propinquity may prove too strong for either his or her
self-control--and what then?

But there is one good side. The girls of to-day are so accustomed to
taking care of themselves, and the men of to-day are so accustomed to
every kind of familiarity at every party, that both are better equipped
to handle a week or two of unrestricted temptation than their parents
and grandparents would have been. If the attraction which draws the
two summer holiday mates together is mental, it is very likely to
continue after the holiday is over--and may end in marriage or lasting
friendship. If it is merely physical--nature is a strong temptress! And
by the end of a fortnight--one or both could very well be left with
regrets.

I should give the same advice that I do about drinking and smoking:
Analyze what are likely to be the consequences of what you are
proposing to do, and then decide if the pleasure and re-creation to be
gained are worth while. Don't run into a difficult situation blindly.
If you decide that they are, then enjoy them yourself--but keep your
head. If the girl has real self-respect she is quite safe in any
situation among civilized people. If she has not--well, then she is
likely to be drawn into scrapes anywhere. It does not require a summer
holiday as a medium!

For the young men the situation is more difficult and would not be
tolerated, of course, by European males, who are of more fiery natures.
But customs of countries alter points of view and if American young men
are trained to stand transitory engagements--or flirtations--without
losing their self-control, then why should not hard-working girls have
the joy of make-believe love affairs to gild with memories the dull
hours of their working year!

Common sense should decide what is worth while!

What should be a young girl's idea of honor? Shakespeare explained it
as, "To thine own self be true, and it must follow, as the night the
day, thou canst not then be false to any man." Long ago I wrote a book
called "Love Itself"--in which I made the heroine, called Halcyone, in
her life and in her religion and in her words, expound this idea of
honor. To her it did not matter in the least what other people thought
of her; it was what she thought in her own soul of herself. She and the
hero talked of the story of Jason and Medea one day when she was but
a little girl--and Halcyone said: "Jason should not have had anything
to do with Medea--that is where he was wrong--but having given her his
word he should have kept it."

The hero replied with a question: "Even though she was a witch?"
Halcyone answered, "It was still his word, don't you see? Her being a
witch did not alter his word. You do not understand the point at all,
it seems. It is not to whom it is you give your word; it is to you it
matters that you keep it, because to break it degrades yourself."

If only this simple principle could be drummed into young people's
heads, so that it held the subconscious mind, causing its reaction to
become automatic, think what a wonderful world would open! A world
where there was no lying, no cheating, no cruelty, no scheming. Because
no one who held this belief could possibly commit any of the actions
which could degrade the soul.

I often wonder what some young girls feel like who have experienced
some of the spiritually degrading familiarities indulged in by modern
youth, when they catch sight of their own faces in the glass in the
morning! Their souls looking back at them through their eyes must
give them a nasty twinge sometimes. None of these have ever even
dimly perceived the true point of honor. They do not fear their own
self-contempt, nor the contempt of any god they may believe in; their
only fear is in being found out and cheapened in the eyes of the
world. If they feel that they have safeguarded this possibility with
cunning they are satisfied. And all not because human nature is really
bad, but because quite simple laws are not explained in youth.

There are two fundamentals which should be kept uppermost in every
young person's mind--the law of self-respect and the law of the
boomerang. These two grasped, the rest of life should be plain sailing.
But as it is, the actions of one year build up the limitations for the
next, and so unhappiness piles itself up.

Is there danger in a girl at business, living by herself away
from home? There is never much danger anywhere if a girl has real
SELF-RESPECT, because there is an unfailing guide in every situation.
But the worst of it is, modern girls have not always this useful
quality, or any great belief in any of the Commandments, except the
eleventh, "Thou shalt not be found out."

Modern girls certainly pin a good deal of faith on this "eleventh
commandment," but often have not the brain to accomplish it, and then
the fat is in the fire! So, before you attempt to live alone, you had
better be sure that you have wings strong enough to fly. Otherwise the
fate we often see among little birds who have been pushed out of the
nest too soon, may be yours. Some cat or other predatory animal may
come along and devour you--or the tempest may drown you or batter or
break your tender bones.

Have you ever watched the frightened eyes of a young blackbird or
thrush--hopping along the ground on its first outing--and if you
have held it in your hand, have you felt its little heart thump as
it struggled to be free? Thousands of girls who have emancipated
themselves too soon, must go through this experience. The success of
living alone depends entirely upon the home training beforehand. If
Claire has never known any discipline and has been accustomed to treat
her parents as naught and indulge during her college days in petting
parties and all the license which takes place in the automobile in the
moonlight drives and then she goes to a little flat when she enters
business, it is not very likely she will respect the flat any more
than she respected the automobile, or the darkened sitting room. Then
she had better keep a firm hold on her senses--and above all, obey the
eleventh commandment! Because the jolly partners may not be so easy
to keep within bounds as they were when they knew that some sort of
eventual entry under the paternal roof was ahead of their Claire.

If a girl has not much self-respect she had better be very worldly
wise. If she has self-respect, the young men will subconsciously know
it, and she will be in no danger and she may have a delightful and
independent life, free to develop her own tastes and likings in her
little lonely home. So it is all up to the girl.

Then there are cases where the girls have had to fend for themselves
always. There is no more danger for them in living alone than at any
other time of their lives. But there are many cares. Where the girl
has self-respect, but a very weak will, and she is led away by her
emotions, the thing to realize before embarking upon a flat by herself
is that whatever temptations she had before to be too kind to her
partners, will be redoubled, and she will have only herself to rely
upon. But this very knowledge is very often good for character and
strengthens it.

If the custom of girls living alone is growing--evolution must wish
it to be that way--and I never go against the spirit of a time. So it
will be like everything else in its beginning--the weaklings will go
under and out--and the strong will make laws unto themselves and live
up to them. Only the one thing which stands out in everything of this
swiftly moving era, is that the duty of drumming right principles into
the heads of their offsprings is the colossal one for parents. And it
is they, not the children, who will be held responsible for after days
by inexorable nature-judgment.

I am receiving such queer letters from people! One person has just
asked me: "Is money an adequate compensation for a girl's broken heart
or engagement?"

I should say that if a girl is so unfine in sentiment that she can
bring her love-hurts into a public court, then money is an ample
compensation for them, and her broken heart can soon be mended.

If a man has ruined a girl's life, or caused her to lose her situation
so that disgrace or great poverty face her--and he is a well-to-do
man--I suppose she is justified, if he is beast enough not to offer
some compensation. The idea is so revolting to me, personally, that I
do not feel that I can give an unbiased opinion about it.

But if there has not been actual damage, but only that he has ceased to
care for her, then I do not think she has any more right to endeavor to
obtain money out of him than he would have in obtaining money out of
her in like case.

Girls must remember that unless there is something in them able to
hold the affection of men, no man can go on caring, once the first
flush of excitement is over. Then it is very hard on the man to have to
go on and keep to the engagement when all emotion has departed. This
very sort of girl who cannot hold a man is the kind that feels very
sentimentally herself--and is more liable to have what is called a
"broken heart" than one who is very attractive.

There are, of course, brutes of men who deliberately make love to one
girl after another, with no intention but to sip the honey of new
flowers--and if one girl more thick-skinned than the rest can make him
pay for it--it is a good thing.

But in a case, we will say, where the girl entered into a liaison
with a man and has not been deceived and decoyed, but went of her
own free will, she ought to have known that such affairs are always
only transient--and when the man's love begins to wane, the money
he will settle upon her ought to be a considerable compensation. No
self-respecting girl who is engaged to a man ought to want him to marry
her if she knows that he is only going through with it for his word's
sake--she ought to free him at once--and never blame him, but rather
question herself to find out why she lost his affection.

Many women are most unjust to men--they think men ought to go on loving
them no matter how they behave, or how unattractive they become--and
feel they have a right to heap reproaches for what is their own fault.
If a man has either an engagement or a liaison with a girl, and he
knows he is growing not to care for her, he is in honor bound to tell
her so, not let her find it out. Then, if she will take money instead
of himself, he had better give it to her.

In the case of the liaison it is his duty, for the girl has chanced her
reputation for him, and he must make provision for her so that she may
not sink. But if money can heal the pains of unrequited love--then the
love has not been very deep! Why not call it something else? I wish
this part which I am always hammering upon were better understood. I
mean that when you lose love it is your fault or misfortune--not the
other person's. If this were understood, it would save so many quarrels
and reproaches, and pride would come to the aid of the deserted ones
and make them pull themselves together. How can you, in logic, blame
people for something over which they have no control?

You cannot love--or grow indifferent--to order; the other person ceases
to draw you, and if you desired a thousand times to go on loving,
you could not make yourself do so. Nor can you become indifferent at
will--while the other person's charm is holding you all the commands
in the world won't detach you from your love. And if a third person is
able to come between loves, it is because he or she has been able to
destroy the loved one's charm in the other's eyes--or is in himself or
herself a strange magnet!

So my advice is--look love and its nature straight in the face with
common sense and you will blame or praise no one but yourself for its
coming or going.

Will business life make girls harder? Or less likely to win a desirable
mate?

I should certainly say that it is making them harder--on the outside.
Does work--manual work, I mean--make the hands harder? Of course it
does, but the nerves under the skin remain sensitive. And so it is
with the mind. Business girls, to be successful, must acquire a certain
amount of callousness, or they would be too tender to buffet with the
world that they have to mix with. Their sense of self-protection has to
be on the alert! They have to be watchful and more or less suspicious;
they have, in short, to put on a suit of armor and if they are not
careful they are apt to wear it when business is over; and then they
become unattractive, just as a man would be unattractive if you saw him
in a pinafore!

Business girls are better able to judge of men than home girls--they
are much less likely to be taken in. And they naturally grow harder,
because they have experiences of how simply horrid some men can be,
who, if they did not know of them in their business aspects, they would
believe to be charming. It is difficult for a man to put it over on a
business girl--and as men really enjoy playing the part of protector
and provider of the helpless, this very acuteness of vision in the
business girl destroys her charm in some men's eyes.

But the arrangement of the present-day world requires that there should
be an immense percentage of business girls--so the wise thing is to
analyze and see how they can keep their charm while they must grow
apparently hardened on the outside.

Having very refined and gentle manners does not take away from ability.
In fact, real ability hidden under femininity is of strong magnetic
attraction. The first thing to guard against--for a business girl--is a
hard manner! Let her feel as self-confident as she pleases, but never
show it in aggressive ways. The moment a male creature senses this
air of superiority he becomes antagonized. If she is much better at a
job than the man she likes is, she must never let him think that she
knows it. Business girls, to be loved, must really play two parts! They
must cover up their tempered steel in velvet cases! And when one of
them loves and marries, she must take care that she only brings her
business ability into the proper ordering of her home--not in a hard
manner of handling her husband. Some weaklings in the way of men like
dictatorial women--but they are not worth having.

A business girl's critical faculties are better trained than a home
girl's. All discipline is good for character--and business discipline
helps many timid and vacillating women to form themselves.

I would say to business girls: "You are fortunate! Now use that part
of your common sense which makes you get on, in studying the nature
of the man who has attracted you, and crush the evidences of hardness
which would put him off." In short, do not take your business manner
into your private life. Because all men's subconscious minds are
still reacting to the belief that woman is a weaker creature who must
be protected and guided--women are doing their best to alter this
creed. And when they do, all tender love will have left the world.
But meanwhile, if they want happiness, they had better fulfill man's
ideal--or let him have a good counterfeit!

There are always new questions cropping up--styles that are loudly
condemned, dances that are labelled sinful, innovations that horrify
people over fifty, and conventions that are thrown to the four winds.
And since one's conduct and appearance has so much to do with the
feelings of the opposite sex, these questions are of vital importance
to all young girls who hope some day to marry the man of their dreams.

Let us take the subject from its broadest point of view--that of new
fads and fashions in general. The first thing to realize is the utter
futility of going against the spirit of the Age. From the earliest days
of civilization, waves of an irresistible desire for some change--some
freer expression of emotion--have periodically swept over society; all
the people with limited horizons of thought have immediately launched
forth their protests, and their horrified and outraged feelings upon
whatever the subject happens to be, have been expressed in frantic
cries. But the spirit of the Age has just laughed at them, and gone
its way and they have either eventually had to fall in with its
mandates, or have been swept aside and left high and dry in loneliness.
I have no space here, or desire to bore you, by giving instances in
the past of what I mean; but to state two--everyone knows the horror
the introduction of the waltz created, when, for the first time men
and women actually embraced while dancing! And the thought of a lady
bicycling would have made your grandmother shudder!

About every fad, every fashion, every new thing which is started, the
wise woman reserves judgment. Because these matters are not questions
of right and wrong, which a sense of duty should direct her to have a
decided opinion upon immediately; they are merely questions of taste
and expediency, and a calm review of them first is necessary before
making up the mind.

If a woman is of a sufficiently distinguished personality, and is
endowed with prestige and great social position, she can start
originalities herself if she pleases. But, if she is a very young
girl, this is most hazardous, and the really sensible thing to do is
to follow the oft-quoted maxim and "wait and see!" It is as foolish to
plunge with ardor into an untested new fad--which you may be ashamed
of presently--as it is to treat it with antagonistic scorn and swear
you will never have anything to do with it! Either course of action may
possibly place you in an undesirable or ridiculous position after a
while, when the fad or fashion has either shown itself to be vulgar and
impossible--or has come to stay!

Give no opinion upon any radically new departure. Quietly and in your
own mind weigh its merits and demerits, and see if they come above or
below the standard of your own self-respect and the true sense of the
fitness of things--and then presently decide for or against.

Never be ruled by the outcries of old-fashioned people any more than
you must be led away by the featherbrains of your own age. But when
you have arrived at the moment for decision, judge the thing itself by
those two standards that I have just indicated, and not by what anyone
else thinks of it. Ask yourself: "If I wear these clothes, or dance
this dance, am I degrading my ideal of myself in any way? Is there
really something indecent and immodest in it? Or is it shrieked at
simply because some of the shriekers are too old to enjoy it, or their
minds have turned to whatever side of it they can fix upon which can be
developed into something suggesting impropriety?"

When you have sifted the motives for the outcries against the new
fashion, whatever it may be, and have come to your own conclusions,
go along steadily on your way, and be not disturbed, remembering
always that excess in anything is undesirable and all eccentricity
is vulgar in a young girl. There will be plenty of unbalanced youths
and maidens in your world who will rush headlong into any new fad the
instant it is suggested to them. Well, be very sagacious! And let them
be the _ballons d'essai_! Watch how the thing seems to you and if it
is likely to lead to pleasure or disgust. You will not have committed
yourself to either side by this abeyance of expressed opinion, and
can be safely "seated upon the fence" for a sufficient time to be able
to decide whether the debated thing is only some small passing folly
of one set--or if it is really something brought about by the spirit
of the Age. You will soon be able to settle this question, and, if you
find that it has this omnipotent force at its back, do not hesitate to
adapt it to your desires, and _use it gracefully_. I have emphasized
these three words on purpose, because therein lies the whole pith of
the subject--for it is so often the manner of a thing which counts more
than the matter.

There is another important fact to be remembered, namely, the
tremendous force of familiarity and custom which can turn startling
innovations into unnoticeable and innocuous every-day occurrences.

If one stops to think for a minute one can conjure up numbers of sights
which, viewed from a detached point uninfluenced by the familiarity of
custom, would seem horribly shocking to one or other of our senses.
For instance, if we had never seen a butcher's shop before, some of
us would faint at the first view of it! This unpleasant simile I give
merely to show you in a very concrete and forcible manner what I
mean--your own intelligence will apply the test to other subjects.

Thus, I remember I went with a party to a very fashionable restaurant
some years ago, where as a rule, only the élite of society
congregate--and where reserve and decorum are the natural tone of the
place. However, for the New Year's Eve feast, it seemed to have opened
its doors to a crowd of the most aspiring inhabitants of Suburbia, who
afterwards danced in the ballroom. They indulged in wonderful "One
Steps" and "Fox Trots." I recollect how we stood and watched them and
laughed at some of the sights. Respectable, and often very plump,
mothers of families, with agonized faces of strain in case they should
forget a step, were bumping against and clinging in strange fashion
to some equally preoccupied partner! I thought then how undignified,
how even revolting it was. But now when I go out, even among the most
_recherchees grandes dames_ and see them (grandmothers some of them!)
taking their hour or two of exercise by dancing the Fox Trot, I am
moved by no spirit of disgust, I merely feel critical as to whether or
no they do it well--so far has custom and familiarity removed antipathy!

So I want you to take this powerful factor into consideration in all
matters of innovations. I want you to realize that they will become
unremarkable and unimportant--so that the only sensible, just, and
sagacious way to look at them, if you should feel you wish to indulge
in them, is to try to find out how far you can do so at that present
moment of the day without making yourself ridiculous or looking
unseemly. You can always exploit and expand your style when you see
it is advisable. As I said before, there is no rigid law of right and
wrong about such affairs, all are weighed by custom and suitability to
present circumstances.

Do not think for a moment, however, that, in all this that I have
been saying, I am advocating a hypocritical course of conduct which
may be applied to other things. This "wait and see" attitude I am only
suggesting as prudent to adopt over such light matters as fashions and
fads.

In choosing an article of dress you must remember the vital matter of
its suitability; suitability generally, suitability for the occasions
you mean to wear it on, its suitability to yourself and your type. If
you cultivate these points and use your eyes and observation to see
what is the prettiest note in passing fashion, you can counteract any
rather commonplace appearance nature may have endowed you with, and
turn it into a quaint, picturesque little individuality.

Never buy things that you do not actually want just because they are
cheap. Cheap things nearly always have disadvantages, or they would
not be cheap. Have few clothes and good ones. Take care of them, and
do not ruthlessly crush and rumple them when you have them on, even
though you have a good maid to repair your ravages afterwards. Be
extremely particular about your foot-covering, too. Never buy any
of the eccentric fashions that you see in every shop window, and on
the feet of every little person trotting in the street. Wear only
the simplest, neatest, and daintiest things made. If the instinct of
personal distinction does not come naturally to you, cultivate it by
observation. Every time you go out observe which women look the nicest
and what makes them achieve this effect. Examine your own, and try to
imagine which of the styles would suit you best and make you look the
least ordinary.

The whole tendency of modern society is toward rowdiness and vulgarity,
and if one is very young and full of spirits, it is so easy to be led
away into indiscretions when one sees most of one's companions doing
the same thing. But this is very foolish, because the least semblance
of rowdiness or vulgarity robs a girl of her dearest charm in the eyes
of worth while men. So I shall be quite ruthless in what I am going to
say.

It is very much the fashion now to lunch and dine at restaurants;
even the most youthful débutantes go to them alone, or to large
boy-and-girl dinners before balls or theater parties, when there may
be only one or two mothers present. I must give you a few hints as to
what I notice is common and unattractive behavior on these occasions.
One can derive a cynical amusement from sitting quietly and watching
the entrance and exit of people in restaurants, so atrocious are the
movements of most of them. It is seldom that anyone seems to remember
that in public true distinction is shown by the quietest and most
dignified bearing.

You will see women and girls flustering in, all self-conscious and
plainly aware that they are being observed. In a public place true
breeding should give you the same repose as at home, and all but
your own personal acquaintances should be apparently unobserved. So
cultivate this unconscious bearing. Finish your toilet before you leave
the dressing-room, and then walk easily and without looking about you
to join your party.

And when you are at the table, do not lounge all over it! If you
have this deplorable modern habit in your own or intimate friend's
houses, for heaven's sake leave it behind you when you come out! To
see a lot of--presumably--ladies lounging all over the cloth, as they
lean forward eagerly to talk to their _vis-à-vis_ or the person next
to them, is not an engaging sight, and only a few years ago it would
have been considered as branding them as belonging to another world.
Whatever laxity of tenue has become habitual in private life, surely
you can realize that it is very cheap to indulge in it in public, and
that the fact that everything is cheap now is no reason for you, who
are starting in life, and wish to be as charming as possible, to follow
the fashion.

Try to walk through halls gracefully, without self-consciousness or
swinging arms; and when the dinner has begun, enter into the spirit of
it, and endeavor to be agreeable to your neighbors, but never forget
that you are in a public place, and that at other tables there are
strangers whom you do not know, and before whom you certainly do not
wish to make yourself of no account. I have seen boy-and-girl parties
at restaurants where, if one had not known the names of the actual
people, one would have presumed they were a set of young hoydens
imagining themselves at a village feast. All noisy or unrestrained
behavior is really very vulgar in any mixed company. I am sure you
will agree with me about this, and, if you will give yourself time to
reflect what self-respect really means, you will discover that, if it
is innate, it will guide you better than any words of mine; and that
even as an acquired quality it makes the only infallible standard to
judge the expediency or inexpediency of certain conduct by.

I wonder if you smoke? There would be no use in my saying that I
personally think it looks utterly unattractive to see a very young girl
puffing her cigarette, because I know that I am old-fashioned and, in
this, have not gone with the times--but such is my opinion. Should
you have not begun to smoke yet, put it off as long as possible, and,
if you do take to it, let it be because you really like it, not for
a pose, as some girls do. If you have acquired the habit already, be
very careful of your teeth as you grow older, and to have your hair
beautifully brushed both night and morning--the smell of stale smoke in
the hair and breath and clothes is so disgusting.

While most modern men are lenient toward the cigarette habit in women,
I am quite sure it is true that a great many of them do not approve of
the practice, especially when a woman has become an addict. Certainly
it does not increase a woman's charm, and since it may lessen her
attraction, and certainly is injurious to her beauty, I am inclined to
warn against the practice.

There is another curious wave over society which renders women less
attractive than they were--a large percentage of them are the seekers,
not the sought-after. They actually hunt men!--the mothers for their
daughters, the girls for themselves--so that the attitude of most
of the modern young men is one of self-defence. They are so sick of
invitations being poured upon them, of being grabbed for this and that,
so wearied with girls flinging themselves at their heads, that their
manners have often become of an insolence that would not have been
tolerated twenty years ago. But who can blame them?

Lots of girls are frightfully eager about their partners, ferreting
them out and reminding them of their engagements. If you have with
modesty shown you are agreeable and desirable to the young men, you
will have aroused their hunting instinct, which is always longing to
find expression, especially nowadays, when they themselves have to play
so often the part of the hunted! If you find yourself not a success,
you must ask yourself why this is so; you must not get nervous about
being left behind, and turn into a seeker!

There are many girls who seem very popular and get plenty of public
attention, but who behave themselves so that they are spoken of lightly
by every young man. Would such popularity be worth having, and what
would it bring in a few years? Not much happiness, I fear. For, even if
one of these girls does marry, she will not have earned the respect of
her husband, nor will she have controlled her own emotions or desires
sufficiently to be able to maintain any stable position in life. When
I look back upon those of this sort that I knew when I was young, I ask
myself where are they now? Some of them are weary old maids--some have
made hole-and-corner, still enduring, wretched marriages--and some have
gone under and are divorced and forgotten. "Look to the end," is an
excellent motto to apply to everything, especially to any common little
pleasure of the moment.

After the first season or two, if a girl does not marry she will have
drifted into one set or another, and you can judge instantly of her
status and prestige by the men she collects around her. If for the
reason of not meeting someone whom you feel you really want to marry,
or for any other reason you should remain free for a while, try at
least to have for your friends only the best and nicest, because, as I
have said again and again, like draws like, and the best is not likely
to be eventually found in the second-best circle.

Do not, as some girls do, look upon society as simply the means to the
securing of a husband, for, although the goal of a sensible girl is
matrimony, still she must come naturally to this state through having,
by her own charm and complete equipment, mental and physical, attracted
a suitable mate; she must not have in front of her marriage as a
necessity, and so be ready to grab any creature who may show himself
willing with her to enter the bond. But again, real self-respect
would ward off any of these dangers, so, if you have it, my advice is
unnecessary.

Supposing, by chance, that your tastes should turn to more serious
matters than just the amusements of balls and games and the pleasures
of your age, never be carried away by any fad or any new idea. Fads are
abnormal, and always show some unbalance. One often hears would-be deep
thinkers announcing platitudes in cant phrases, and they frequently
influence the young and impressionable. You have often, for instance,
heard them making remarks about the "Rights of Man." Now, ask yourself
a commonsense question: What are the Rights of Man? You will find that
the answer is that there are no such things! Man has evolved, and
certain civilizations have conceded him certain privileges, but as he
made no bargain with the Creator when he entered the world he cannot
possibly have any "rights." Servants have "rights" because they are
doing specified work for food and wages--they have made a bargain.
All human beings have "rights" between themselves when they make an
agreement of exchange. But man--just man in the abstract--can have no
"rights" at all, for with whom did he make a bargain? From whom can he
claim them? So, when you hear people using this phrase, you may know
that they are talking balderdash and have not thought about the matter.

Woman has no "rights" either. The whole aspect of these things for
woman is largely a question of geography, climate, and custom. One
might say the only natural "right" a woman appears to have is to become
a mother, because this seems to be her obvious mission in the scheme of
things. But the necessities of civilization and the laws of her country
have, above all things, restricted for her this privilege, except under
certain given circumstances laid down by law. So you see, when you
come to analyze this phrase of "rights" it all falls to pieces! I have
only referred to it by chance, as an illustration of the folly of using
cant phrases.

Never pretend to be clever in any way; be natural and easy, with that
trained ease which is the highest attribute of breeding.

Another defect girls often have which drives desirable men away is
shyness, and very few people stop to analyze its cause. Shyness, when
we have got down to the bedrock of it, is pure personal egotism.
People are shy because they fancy others are observing them. If they
were not so conscious of themselves they would not be obsessed with
this idea; they would realize that they are probably not really very
interesting, and may never have struck others' consciousness at all.
But no--the perpetual, ever-present perception of self makes them
awkward, makes them wonder what effect they are producing, makes them
nervous and the prey of every foolishness. Whereas, if they were not so
sensitively occupied with their own feelings, they would do natural
things without a tremor. I have no patience when I hear a woman being
excused for stiffness and brusqueness by the plea of, "Oh, she is so
dreadfully shy!" It is not real humility--real humility would not be
conscious of self at all. It is vanity and egotism; and when seen in
a grown woman casts a very poor reflection upon those who had the
charge of her bringing-up from earliest childhood. If you are shy, take
yourself sternly to task, analyze what makes you so, and overcome it.
Bashfulness and shyness are as great faults as boldness, and perhaps
cause more unhappiness. The antithesis of shyness is bumptiousness, and
this also comes from egotism; it is a different expression of the same
fundamental fault. Try to eradicate the root if you have a tendency to
either of its demonstrations.

There are all sorts of modern philosophers (in petticoats mostly, but
still some of them are men!) who, with more or less subtle reasoning,
are trying to inculcate an idea of the necessity of individualism in
all women. They urge every unit to express her individuality, with
the result that the average female, who is little higher than the
animal world in intelligence, and not half so endowed with instinct, is
becoming a perfect bore! She has not the sense to see that, if she were
really gifted, nothing on earth could keep her from being individual,
and that, if she is not so, to try to push forward her commonplace
ideas only serves to make her less attractive in the eyes of the
opposite sex. Numbers of foolish featherbrains, bitten with the idea
that they have this high mission of showing their individuality, have
upset all possibility of their own happiness. The mass of women could
not have been intended to be individual by the laws of nature--not
of man--and the few who are highly gifted have unconsciously been
raised on pedestals without their own effort. These are the first to
comprehend that it is necessary to look facts straight in the face, and
to realize that when it comes to the last stand, no matter what laws
are made, man will still be the master, through physical force. And oh!
it would be perfectly frightful, would it not, if we got back to a
state where men were obliged to club us to get their own way!

Few things make a woman more attractive to a man than good manners. The
wise woman gives more than a passing thought to this important subject.

The tendency of the present day is to do away with all gentle things,
and among them courtesy has gone by the board, so that to see anyone
still with beautiful and gracious manners is a thing to be remarked
upon and rejoiced over.

The modern young woman is so innately selfish that, as a rule, her
manners are only good when some definite momentary gain to herself
makes their display worth while. She is too short-sighted to look ahead
and see their value, and she is no longer a proud person remembering
what is due to herself, and, therefore, that good manners ought to be
the stamp of her breeding. She is often as primitive as a young savage,
with a smattering of a fair mental education on top.

Numbers of kind-hearted mothers about forty years ago began to think
that their own training has been horribly stiff and cruel, and gave
a much greater license to their offspring. Ridicule was cast upon the
rules that had been in practice for every girl entering society. People
began to laugh at numbers of things, a sense of humor was reviving,
and it attacked the methods and fashions of "young ladyhood." The
children of those days, who are now mothers of the present young girls,
went a step further, with the best intentions, and augmented, by the
craze for exercise and out-of-door games, the effect of the lax rules
of deportment, so that now one hardly ever sees a really gracious
and graceful young girl, and some of them are the most unattractive
specimens of youthful females in consequence.

The wise woman will be a cunning creature and combine the methods of
the old and the new. If your tastes incline to violent outdoor games,
assiduously cultivate beautiful and gracious manners as well, so that
the young men you play with, while admiring your skill, will not feel
that they can treat you as "another fellow," hardly with courtesy, and
with no consideration.

Try not to be ungraceful in walking. Try not to sit in every awkward
position that may be comfortable. Do not display yards of ankle. One
sees these defects so constantly now that one has almost ceased to
remark upon them.

If you want really to enjoy life and count as a coveted quantity, you
must rise above the general company of young, unmeaning beings of your
sex, so as to make the nice young man you may fancy think of you, not
as one of a batch for him to choose from, but as the only desirable
creature in all the world for him to strive to obtain. The really
interesting thing is to be a personality, not one of the herd. And all
the stuff and nonsense which you may have been told about women fitting
themselves for a self-sufficing existence, and their "rights," and
their assertion of equality, are pitiful makeshifts, of use only if the
poor things do not obtain the sole real joy and happiness--to be the
loved and honored mate of some nice man. If, by your self-assertion
and exaggerated mentality, you have been able to crush out all sex
instinct, then you become as the working bee--of a third sex, an
anomaly in nature, and a ridiculous excrescence in God's scheme of
human progression. So for heaven's sake keep this in view. Train what
individuality in yourself you will, but keep your clear perspective so
as to be able to see the ultimate goal of happiness.

I think I have been rather generalizing, so now I want to come down
to a concrete description of what I think would be a perfect young
girl, and you must tell me if you agree with this picture of a female
"admirable Crichton"! I think, firstly, she ought to be sensible enough
to understand the colossal importance and value of beauty, and to have
learned to take care of her personal appearance, so that in every way
she is a pleasure to the eye. She ought to have discovered early what
style of garments suits her; she should have practiced until she can
do her hair becomingly; and by exercises, and by care in remembering
what is ugly and to be avoided, she should have perfected the grace of
her body's movements. All these things have been looked upon, not as
vanities, but as the natural polishing of the body God had entrusted
her with, as the shrine for her soul.

Her voice should be soft, and her cultivation at least
sufficient--should she not be naturally clever--to make her know the
topics of the day which are interesting to converse upon; and she
should be broad enough not to be prejudiced about any of them.

Unselfishness in her should go as far as not to want always to have her
own way, regardless of whom it hurts or discomforts. (One could not
expect more than that in these days!)

She ought to have so high a respect for herself that she could never
make herself cheap, but she should also have common sense enough to
realize that she must have her weapons of attraction peculiarly well
polished. Then, out of the circle of possible husbands she will have to
choose from, she may hope to attract the best--because like clings to
like.

As she is my ideal young girl, she will not be stupid enough to set
out with the idea of making her own life self-sufficing. Whatever
circumstances may force her to do afterward, at least to start with
she will know that to be happily married is the natural goal, and that
to obtain this good thing she must take care of her equipments and fit
them for the post she aspires to.

She must have tact and a highly cultivated sense of humor, so that she
may not be a bore with her notions and her egotism. She must not stand
against the times, but be so ruled by fine taste that she cannot be
drawn into any exaggeration.

Her ambition is to become the inspiration and adored mate of whatever
nice man she may marry, because, as she is very highly refined and
balanced, she will not be attracted by the weakling or the fool, whom
she would inevitably rule while she despised him.

If she finds that somehow she has drifted into union with one of
these beings, then it will be time enough for her to assert her
supremacy--and the more self-controlled and equilibrated she is, the
more successfully will she be able to stand alone if necessity requires
her to do so. But, remember that the natural goal and the happy and
glorious goal of a woman is to strive to be the refining influence,
the inspiration and the worshiped joy of a man. When she has to be
self-sufficing, then, no matter how great she may become, the happiness
is only second-best. So as you have youth and a clear sky, you should
set forth with a desire for this best and greatest happiness.

You must not forget that whatever the other person thinks and feels
about you is what you yourself have given him the presentment of. It
lies entirely with you, therefore, what impression on his heart and
brain you wish to create. I do assure you that it is infinitely more
agreeable when he thinks you all that is perfect, and is passionately
in love, than when he is mildly attracted by your golf and your
camaraderie, while his unemployed senses, left at liberty to roam,
stray to the more cunning young women, who have realized that some
feminine allurements are not bad things to cultivate. By all means play
your golf and your tennis if they give you pleasure, but try and make
your partner feel that these things are a means to securing the end he
desires: namely, your company and companionship; not that you are the
means to his enjoyment of the game. Do not throw away all mystery and
appear a loud, jolly schoolboy, because, if you do, naturally the other
"boys" will treat you as one of themselves, or as a sister--not as
"another fellow's sister," to be considered, and whose favors are to be
schemed for.

There are all sorts of points about manners which add to a girl's
charm. When you come into a room pay respect to elder people; it will
not take up much of your time, and is a gracious tribute of youth to
age. And when you go out to dine or lunch do not sit silent if you
happen to be bored with the person who is next to you. You owe it
to your hostess to try to make things as agreeable as possible. And
when you are invited to people's houses remember this also: You have
been asked because the hostess likes you, or you are a credit to her,
or she is under some obligation to return some civility from your
family. In all three cases you ought to make good by proving you are
a most desirable guest. Try to acquire prestige, so that none of the
nicest parties are complete without you; then you can choose which
you prefer to go to. But prestige is not acquired without tact and
perfect manners on all occasions. The tendency of all modern society is
toward vulgarity and display, with a ruthless, cynical, brutal worship
of wealth, snatching at any means to the end of luxury and pleasure.
People accept invitations from those they despise, for no other reason
than because they are rich and the entertainment will be well done. It
is awfully cheap, is it not?

Try to be kind to everyone you come in contact with and make them
feel at home, however humble they may be, if they are your guests; be
gracious and thoughtful for their comfort and pleasure--you need never
be familiar or gushing. Be simple and modest; all pretense is paltry
and all boasting is vain; nothing but the truth lasts or gains any
respect.

I should like to tell you a little story before I finish this chapter,
as an instance of really exquisite manners.

A long time ago I was staying in Scotland with a very great lady; we
were all going in to Edinburgh for the day. My friend was a little
short-sighted, and while we stopped at the bookstall before crossing
over the viaduct to the departure platform I noticed a rather
humble-looking little woman nervously and anxiously trying to bow to my
hostess who did not perceive her. After we had mounted the stairs and
crossed the line her daughter told my great lady of this, and how Mrs.
Mackenzie, the new doctor's wife, had looked quite hurt. My friend was
so distressed that she made an excuse to return to the bookstall, so
that she might casually pass the little woman again and bow and speak,
but not to hurt her feelings by making her feel she had done it on
purpose. I went with her, and while buying an extra paper she glanced
up sweetly at the humble-looking little woman, and said:

"Oh! how do you do, Mrs. Mackenzie? I hope your little children are
well, and the Doctor; so glad to see you are quite recovered from the
influenza I heard you had," and then, with a gracious smile, she drew
me on, and we had to run back up the stairs to be in time for our
train. Such manners as these are the only true and beautiful ones,
because they spring from a kind and tender heart.




CHAPTER IV

WINNING AND HOLDING THE WOMAN YOU LOVE


I suppose most of you read the novel "Babbitt," popular some time ago.
It is not about the book that I want to talk, but about the "Babbitts"
of this world. I see dozens of men in the streets and in the hotel, in
appearance exactly like the original Babbitt. That is, much too fat,
with necks as though they were bursting out of their collars, ungainly
figures, faces often badly shaved if it is in the morning, and with
every sign that food and drink and want of proper exercise have choked
the human body with useless deposits.

They vary in age from thirty to fifty, when men ought to be in the
perfection of manhood. Some seem to be growing "Babbittish" even as
young as twenty-five!

Now this is a great tragedy and ought to be combated by women. What is
the first step taken by the splendid young athletes just emerged from
college which begins to turn them into these unappetizing lumps of
human tissue? Women ought to ask themselves these questions:

Is it too many hours sitting in offices? Is it frantic preoccupation
about the almighty dollar? Or what is it? And how can it be altered?
For undoubtedly the women of America are the most beautiful and the
most dainty of any in the world, and it is not fair to them, when one
of them has married a handsome young man for love, that before ten
years have gone he should have turned into a meaty "Babbitt." I am
not speaking here of the character of the "Babbitts," but of their
appearance.

How can these unattractive men expect that their wives can go on loving
them and giving willing service in the home with every atom of romance
knocked out of their dreams? Instead of a fine young lover coming back
from his work--a creature growing grosser each year presents himself!
How can such a man, if he be single, expect to win the love of any
really worth while woman?

Surely to keep himself fit and lean and athletic ought to matter a
great deal more to the ordinary man than it seems to.

There must be a first step in decline. Let us analyze what it
probably is. The fundamental cause, of course, is some want of
self-respect--yes, that sounds drastic, but it is true. If self-respect
were supreme, as it ought to be, no man could permit the inertia of
mind and body which is the initial cause of coarsening. He would feel
that this weakness was not due to his proud spirit, and he would combat
the first sign of it. But very few people have real self-respect--they
think that they have--they throw dust in their own eyes over all their
own feebleness or failings. The first time one of these gorgeous young
men of three or four and twenty perceives in his looking-glass that his
image is thickening, or that his eyes look less clear, or his skin
less healthy, he ought to ask himself what is the probable cause--and
how he can prevent its recurrence.

His scales ought to warn him every month or two, and the size of his
collars. Then he might usefully tell his reflection in the mirror what
he thinks of it! He might wonder if it is the kind of a one which would
be likely to hold the fancy of women in the abstract, and so of the
particular woman in his own case. And then, when he is quite certain
in his own mind about it, he would have the incentive to use practical
means to remedy his defects.

I know there are thousands of women who will feel with me upon this
matter--that is, the tragedy of these fine young men so soon turning
into these unattractive creatures. So supposing that just one of the
"Babbitts" has chanced to look in his mirror and realized that he has
let slip marvelous things--youth and health--and he is beginning to
wonder how he can get them back and so make himself attractive to women.

He ought to first realize that whatever he puts into his body has some
effect upon it--bad or good. Then he might review what, in a general
way, he has been eating in the last few years, and whether or not his
common sense approves of it. Especially he might try to remember what
he has been drinking, and what kind of an effect the various forms
of alcohol would logically be likely to create upon the coats of his
interior mechanisms.

And, lastly, he might speculate upon how much nicotine poisoning
could have permeated his blood and his glands and his system from the
hundreds of cigars and cigarettes he has indulged in.

He might put a little of his clever business calculation into all this,
as though he were investigating a new business he had taken over which
had been a failure and which he meant now to make into a success. If
he were to do this frankly and honestly, without camouflaging truth
to his vanity, in the space of a year he could regain a very great
deal of his lost attraction. But human beings, unless they can see
instantaneous results of any actions, invariably attribute causes to
whatever is least wounding to their self-love. So our Mr. Babbitt of
thirty-five--weighing many pounds more than he ought to--will tell
himself the change is caused by things he cannot help--exigencies
of his work, worry, scarcity of time, heredity, and so on. But
he will not readily face the primary causes which he could have
helped--self-indulgence in food, in alcohol, and in nicotine!

A young man of twenty-eight, who once was very handsome, deplored that
he was losing all his hair and his figure. I could see that his figure
was no longer that lithe, lean, spruce shape it had been when he left
his university. His physical attraction was really gone. He gave every
imaginable reason for these alterations and misfortunes--none, of
course, that he could possibly have avoided. He smelt very unpleasant
as we talked--and he smoked cigarettes incessantly, and delightedly
told me of the marvelous parties he attended continually where he got
saturated with alcohol--"plastered," he called it!

When I ventured to say these two things might be the cause of his
changed appearance and thinning hair, he was indignant. It was
indigestion--and having to wear a hat so much in his business!

Well, the thing is hopeless with minds like that. It means that the
person is a weakling who lets his habits rule him, not he his habits.

Then I thought of his dainty, charming young wife of twenty-five--with
her care of her person, her pretty, slim figure, her fresh, clean
teeth--she does not smoke or drink--and I did not wonder that she looks
often sad and restless and is beginning to be interested in other men.
They have been married five years and Bruce has turned from a young
Greek god into a rather disgusting person. If his wife ever _does_ grow
to care more for someone else than she does for him, it will be his own
fault--and he will have no one to blame but himself.

Now it is quite useless to be a crusader and hammer at human follies.
The only possible way to help humanity is to dispel ignorance upon
the subjects, then leave it to each person's free will to change
or not, as he or she thinks best. If people knew what their tissues
and organs looked like, because of the things they pour over and
into them, and knew exactly what would follow in their health and
appearance, thousands of them would alter their mode of life and food
and drink--but they don't know, and so drift on! What a pity!

The "Babbitts" often have hideous haircuts, where the head is almost
shaved above the back of the neck and above the ears, while the rest of
the hair is left longer, suggesting to unaccustomed eyes that they are
all suffering from some unpleasant skin disease requiring treatment. Or
that they are trying to look like Chinamen--but without the pig-tails!

The American barber shops, men of other nations tell me, are the best
in the world, with every known appliance for rendering male creatures
"soigne." Then why do we not see better results?

No man can want to be grotesque and unattractive. Why, then, not pay
more attention to appearance, health and exercise?

Fat men may be good dancers, excellent dinner companions, and jolly
friends--but they cannot successfully play the rôle of lover. They are
romantic--they do not appeal to most women.

So take warning, you men who measure more around the waist than around
the chest! If you want to be successful in love you must eat less or
exercise more--or both.

What money can make up for rolls of fat in the wrong places,
indigestion, and inertia? To amass less dollars would seem to be common
sense and keep health and vigor to enjoy what you do possess.

Men seldom continue to love women who become revolting objects, so why
should they expect women to go on loving them if they make no effort to
please the eye?

A good plan, when you happen to be dining in a restaurant, is to try to
imagine what each person, including yourself, will look like in five
years. Really take yourself to task and discover what road you are
going upon and what its end is likely to be. It will often give you a
salutary jolt. But remember that nothing remains stationary. Therefore
with will it is perfectly possible to improve all the time, just as
with drift you are certain to go down hill faster each year.

But above everything, the splendid young men ought to determine that
they will remain fit and lithe, and continue to take the necessary
exercise which keeps them so.

Now, this is enough about the outsides of the Babbitts! I want to talk
about their characters. They are all probably like the Babbitt in the
book--kind-hearted fellows, with a longing for Romance tucked away
somewhere under their over-fed and over-drunk exteriors.

If you have a longing for something in a shop, or a position, or any
material thing, you usually use your brains to see how you can secure
it. And you generally do get it. But romance will not come to you, if,
far from using your brains to see what will bring it, you just drift
on, piling up conditions which will absolutely prevent its reaching you.

Romance is that joy of the spirit which gilds ordinary things into
radiance, and if poor Mr. Babbitt had realized that he had it in his
grasp when he started out on life's journey with his mate, but that he
would lose it--certainly if he were not very careful--he would have
used more perspicacity.

Girls could prevent their sweethearts from turning into
Babbitt-husbands if they determined to; but the sad part is that many
of them become Babbitt-wives; forgetting all the little refinements
of reserve in daily life which it is necessary to observe to avoid
disillusion.

The Mrs. Babbitt came to wake the reluctant Babbitt from his dream
of romance--combing out her switch of false hair! If only all girls
starting out on their honeymoons would remember that no man can remain
in love if he is disillusioned, and all you men would remember that no
girl can continue to feel thrills if she is disgusted, thousands of
unions would be much happier in after years.

But no man need grow into a "Babbitt" because this condition is not the
inevitable reaction of attention to business and a boring, small-town
life! It is caused by an attitude of mind, a defective sense of
understanding what effect your own habits and personal ways may have on
others.

What man, if he took time to think or had fine sensibilities,
could possibly clear his throat and spit in the incredible way one
unfortunately bears and sees so many of them doing? Think of the
feelings of these dainty girls these spitters may be taking out! Think
of the poor wives who hear these noises in their walks with their
husbands. If men are too unrefined to notice these things themselves,
their womenkind--be they mothers, sisters, sweethearts, or wives--ought
to tell them how hideously unattractive the habit is. They should not
let it go on.

Of course, in other countries where class distinction still exists
strongly none of these things are ever seen except among the quite
low classes, because they would not be tolerated in any grade calling
itself society.

But in a democracy, where everyone is equal, it is more necessary than
ever to encourage refinement universally, because the road mender or
sewer cleaner may one day be a millionaire and the head of a vast
business. So it is useful for him to have good manners and refined ways
from the beginning drummed into his head.

As stated above, in a democracy everyone is supposed to be equal. It
is, of course, merely a phantasy. No law or self-assertion can make
equality, because what is in the brain of one man or woman may make
him or her soar above the crowd. Brain will always assert itself. A
master mind will rule and a servant mind will be ruled, whether under
Bolshevism or Autocracy.

The best thing a democracy can do is to provide education so as to
raise the average standard and to give the chance of obtaining mental
equipment to all alike.

But I don't want to discuss democracy--but to talk of conquering those
bad habits that make one unattractive and revolting to the opposite
sex. It is much more amusing to be a "Master" than a "Servant," in
your inner being. A "Servant" may have millions of dollars and dozens
of retainers but cannot command one of his own weaknesses. He is a
miserable slave to a habit--or a desire! A "Master" may not possess a
cent--but his actions and desires are under his own iron control, and
because of this, he very seldom remains centless long!

Few human beings are entire masters; all of us are apt to allow some
desire or habit, now and then, to get the better of us. But strong
characters conquer nearly every time. A respected head of a woman's
club, who believed she ruled everything and everybody, told me last
week she just couldn't resist having cups of coffee throughout the
day. "I'm a regular coffee-fiend," she said with pride. And when I
said, "Poor Servant!" she was most annoyed, even when I explained that
she was but a servant to her coffee habit. Men are often servants to
smoke, or to drink, or to gambling.

It is want of real self-respect that made this club leader drink too
much coffee--and be a servant to her habit. It is want of self-respect
which makes people say: "I just had to," and "I must have it." If the
inward you is considered by the conscious you, as something which must
not be soiled, something that is King, which your pride must keep as
such, you would never permit any folly or degradation. You would not
care what praise you received as a reward if you knew it was not due to
you, nor would you be concerned by undeserved blame.

If all bad habits in men were recognized by women as the disgusting
and revolting things they are, and an individual protest made by every
offended girl, in a year or two the habits would be eradicated.

The female population are to blame for putting up with horrid things.
Girls are to blame for tolerating young men who are unrefined. Wives
are to blame for allowing their husbands to form sickening habits.
Mothers are to blame for permitting their little sons to acquire ways
they know will be revolting when they grow up. The small amount of
effort it would take men to be refined and attractive would be repaid
a million-fold in general happiness, and would certainly cause love
between the sexes to last much longer.

I wonder how many romances owe their ends--if the truth were known--to
the man having revolted the senses of sight, scent or hearing of the
woman! For never forget the love emotion is entirely dependent upon the
reaction created upon the senses.

Affection can continue through offended senses, but not the love
thrill. The most faithful spirit cannot feel responsive to passion if
the other person smells unpleasant or is revolting to sight or hearing.

So, girls, do be very particular in yourselves, and absolutely exact
refinement and delicacy in your sweethearts!

Much of the situation is in your own hands.

How can a man keep his wife a sweetheart?

Now that is not a difficult question to answer, but a very difficult
thing to get a man to do! Because one of the most tiresome wants about
human beings is that they seem to have a defective sense of values, and
are constantly putting stress upon the wrong thing.

As for a man's wishing to keep his wife a sweetheart, he might possibly
really desire it more than anything else in his life. But this
defective realization of values would make him do all the things which
disillusion women, just because the man has ordinary habits, and he had
never paused to calculate what reaction they would have on his mate.
This question of keeping a legal partner surrounded with the glamour
which differentiates duty from inclination, is primarily concerned with
the senses; if they are affronted, the emotion departs.

So, Jack, the first thing you have got to ask yourself is: Are you
sure the knowledge of that wedding ring, and the consequent sense of
security may not make you careless of the little things you were
careful about when you were only engaged? Will you be as particular as
to your cleanliness and apparel as you ought to be? Will you remain
appetizing altogether? Because if you do not, you cannot expect Claire
to feel thrilled by you--with the best will in the world to do so, her
physical senses will be offended and she cannot help feeling revolted.

It is not that she is fickle or unkind; it is not that you are less
loving or have grown selfish. It is just because the senses of sight
and scent and hearing will have received an unpleasant shock--and then,
alas! Glamour cannot continue, reality chases it away!

We are not supposed to talk of such indelicate things, but when an ill
has to be mended there is no use in giving receipts or attributing
causes to untrue reasons. Nine times out of ten the cause that has made
the wife cease to be the sweetheart is some want of refinement in the
man. Women are sensitive creatures, and these things matter to them
exceedingly! It is a continual source of surprise to me, for instance,
at restaurants where they dance, to see these perfectly exquisite
girls who have taken the trouble to bathe and change into pretty
fresh clothes for the evening, being clasped in the arms of young men
obviously in their business suits--hot and ungroomed, and probably not
bathed since morning!

I am coming back to this subject later, but I had to touch upon it here
because it has a strong bearing upon the sweetheart-wife question.
If you want to keep your Claire the former, Jack, remember first to
make yourself physically attractive and then remember to go on being a
lover in all ways. Remember to SAY nice things, to show appreciation,
to express your satisfaction with Claire and her ways if you feel it.
Do not satiate her with caresses at the wrong time. Make yourself
interesting, and attract what mind she may have, but on the other hand,
do not relapse into coldness because that may have been your natural
character, and you were only ardent while the hunting instinct was
unsatisfied.

If Claire puts on pretty things to please you, do not take them as a
matter of course; notice and admire them. If she, on her side, begins
to be careless, or shows unreasonableness, do not put up with it. Let
her know immediately that you have remarked these defects, and that she
will disillusionize you if they go on! Her vanity will then make her
very careful, and you will want her as a sweetheart as much as she will
want you.

Why be so illogical as to imagine a wife will remain a sweetheart if
you are not a lover? You would never be so stupid in a business deal!
Human relationship can be disgusting, or it can be as beautiful as the
angels. The difference is not in the force of the re-creative instinct,
but in the spiritual refinement of the individuals. And if spiritual
and physical refinements are not there, no wife can remain a sweetheart.

It is the rarest thing in the world to find an American girl
anything but prettily dressed, even if it has to be inexpensively,
sometimes--and, rarer still, not to find her spruce and clean and fresh
looking. In the evening she gives the impression that she has taken
pains to go home and wash and dress and re-do her hair; she is, in
short, the most appetizing young woman in the entire world.

Why do the men then not appreciate her sufficiently to take equal
pains? Why do they soil this lovely thing by dancing with it in
business coats--and shirts obviously crumpled with the heat of the day?

I do not care how poor the young man is, he can make the same effort
his pretty partner does--nor does it matter how old the coat is, if
it is not the same as he has worn all day. It would not matter if
the shirt were frayed--if it were fresh as Claire's gown. It is not
showing proper respect for the girl he is taking out to be so ungroomed
looking. Claire's sight and scent nerves are absolutely revolted by an
unkempt appearance in an escort--so how can she really enjoy herself?
This uncouthness in young men is especially pitiable since they are
often such splendid specimens of humanity. If all the lovely girls in
each city were to band together and refuse to go out with the boys
until they were bathed and dressed after business--what a difference
it would make! I do not mean that they must put on dinner coats and
white waistcoats if they have all driven out to some country club and
the girls are in afternoon frocks. But in the cases where the girls
have put on smart evening dresses with bare necks and arms, the young
men should take the same trouble. Why should women have to make all
the effort? No European girls, if they were as beautifully turned out
and lovely as the girls are here, would put up with uncouth young men
for one moment. You never see the anomaly of men in business suits and
women in ballroom dresses. It would not be considered respectful to the
women.

So why should American girls, who are by far the loveliest in the
world, submit to it? To be difficult--"difficile," as the French
say--about these things, would only add to their value. And if the
young men would give them less expensive food when they go out and
spend the money on making themselves fresh and well dressed, I am sure
the girls would appreciate it. I dare say the young men will be very
cross with me for saying this--but I do not care! I am all for the
girls in it. I am their champion, and many of them have written to me
and asked me to air this subject and their grievance.

How can Claire feel romantic--when her fresh pink georgette form is
being clasped in thick tweed arms! Half her force is taken up in
controlling her disgust at the odor of the many times damped wool.
How can love-light thrill through her eyes when they look up and see
a thatch of hair above an almost shaven ring of head, making the ears
stand out like a monkey's? Of course she cannot, poor, dear, beautiful
child! She is disappointed, and she does not know why. The very sense
which taught her to make herself so _soignee_ and refined makes her
sensitive. To keep the body bathed and groomed and brushed and spruce
is the duty of every self-respecting youth.

Girls, don't you agree with me?




CHAPTER V

KEEPING A MAN IN LOVE


No one accomplishes any rôle successfully without some preparatory
training--and the rôle of a married woman requires a good deal of
thought bestowed upon it before it should be undertaken.

We will suppose that you desire to retain the love and devotion of
your husband, and have not merely married him for a home and a place
in society. In this case face the fact that it is always a difficult
matter for a woman to keep a man in love with her when once she
belongs to him and he has no obstacles to overcome. For man is a
hunter naturally, and when the quarry is obtained his interest in that
particular beast wanes, although the interest in securing by his skill
another of the same species remains as active as ever.

The wise woman realizes all primitive and deep-seated instincts in
human nature, and adapts herself to them. She recognizes the futility
of trying to make her personal protest effective against what is a
fundamental characteristic of all male animals.

Who, seeing a wall with several gates in it, would be so foolish as to
fling herself against the stones instead of quietly going through one
of the openings, simply because she resented the wall's being there
at all! And yet this is what numbers--indeed the majority--of women
do, figuratively, in their dealings with men; and so destroy their own
happiness.

If you decide to try to keep your husband, attend to your means of
attraction. While you were engaged to him you would not have allowed
him to see you looking ugly or unappetizing for the world--such
things are even more important after you are married. Never under
any circumstances let him have the chance of feeling physically
repulsed--for the very first time he experiences this sensation that
will be the beginning of the end of his being _in love_ with you,
although he may go on treating you in a very kind and friendly way.
But if you want to keep him in a blissful state, attend far more to
pleasing his eye and his ear when alone with him than to pleasing
the world when you go out. Let him feel that whatever admiration you
provoke--and the more you do provoke the better he will love you--your
most utterly attractive allurements are reserved as special treats for
himself alone. Very deep affection can remain when all sense of "being
in love" is over, but it has lost its exquisite aroma of sweetness.

Above all, remember this: Though your husband may have the most
honorable desire to be faithful to you in the letter and the spirit, he
cannot by his _own will_ suppress or increase his actual emotion toward
you, and if you destroy his ideal of you it cannot be his fault if his
ardor cools. That is one point of gigantic importance which I always
want to hammer into people's heads--whatever a person thinks or feels
about you, you yourself are responsible for. You have given his or her
sensibilities that impression, exactly as when you look in a mirror
your reflection is reproduced.

People complain of being misunderstood, but it is because they
themselves, unconsciously perhaps, have given the cause for
misunderstanding. You may say a man is a brute and a false traitor,
because in May he was passionately loving, making every vow to you,
but in October he had cooled, and by December he had become in love
with some one else! Granted that some men have fickle natures and more
easily stray than others, still the actual emotion for a particular
person is not under any human being's control, only the demonstrations
of it. I must be very explicit about this statement in case you
misunderstand me.

I mean that no man or woman can love or unlove at will--(by "love" I
mean all the emotions which are contained in the state called "being in
love"). This state in man or woman is produced, as I said before, by
some attraction in the loved one, just as a needle is attracted by a
magnet. If the magnetic power were to lessen in the magnet the needle
could not prevent itself from falling away from it--or if another and
stronger magnet were placed near the needle it would be drawn to that.
It--the needle--would only be obeying natural laws and therefore would
not be responsible.

Which, then, could you blame--the original magnet or the needle?

Obviously the magnet is responsible.

You may reply, "But the magnet did not wish to lessen in attraction;
that and the arrival of the stronger magnet were pure misfortunes and
accidents of fate."

Granted--but this only brings in a third influence--it does not _throw
the blame upon the needle_. So I want you to understand, that if a
man ceases to love you it is your own fault--or misfortune--never
his fault; just as, if you cease to love the man, it is his fault or
misfortune, not yours.

These are truths which ninety-nine women out of a hundred do not care
to face. But the wise hundredth, realizing that she is the magnet,
tries her uttermost to keep her magnetic power strong enough to
withstand all misfortune or the attacks of other magnets--that is, if
she wishes to keep the man who is the needle.

And if he leaves her she must ask herself _how she is in fault_. She
must _never blame him_. If she cannot discover that she is in fault at
all, she is then in the position of the first magnet--and it is her
misfortune; but misfortune can be turned into success by intelligence,
and, with skill, a magnet can be recharged.

Now do you clearly understand this argument? I hope so, because I
have put it plainly enough to make you conscious of your personal
responsibility in the matter of being able to retain your husband's
love. So we can get back to the subject of the vital importance of
keeping his senses pleased with you.

There are numbers of girls who at the end of a month of marriage
have done, said, and looked things which they would have died rather
than let their fiancés perceive, hear, or see, and yet who are much
astonished and feel resentful and aggrieved because they begin to reap
the harvest of their own actions in the fact of their husbands showing
less love and respect for them.

How illogical! How foolish!

The magnet and the needle simile works both ways. If your husband
ceases to draw your affection he will only have himself or his
misfortune to blame--not you. We have been speaking of emotions
hitherto, and of their impossibility of control--and to leave the
discussion at that would open a dangerous door to those featherbrains
who never, if they can help it, look at the real meaning of an
argument, but adapt it and turn it to fit their own desires. So I must
forcibly state that, although the actual emotion in its coming or going
is not under human control, the demonstration of it most emphatically
is, being entirely a question of will. A strong will can master any
demonstration of emotion, and it is the duty of either the young
husband or wife to sternly curb all vagrant fancies in themselves,
whose encouragement can only bring degradation and disaster.

No knowledge of the fact that your husband cares more for you than you
do for him ought to make you lessen your determination to be attractive
to him. To be absolutely unkind or cruel would not have so alienating
an effect as to be unattractive. No woman can count upon her power
if she ceases to charm the man's senses. Should you be happy enough
to love a little less than your husband, you may feel that all this
analyzing of cause and effect which I have been treating you to does
not altogether apply in your case, but still, if you _are wise_ you
will take to heart most of it, and so hold what you have won.

Now for a very important subject: The permitting and encouraging of
vagrant fancies. In these days, when no discipline has been taught
girls, and very little principle, young wives are prone to indulge
any caprice which comes into their heads. Good-looking and attractive
young women are bound to have many temptations to look elsewhere for
diversions very soon after they are married. And here wisdom--quite
apart from high principle--should teach you to resist as much as
possible, because of the end. Ask yourself if it is worth while to
start a ball rolling which can only roll down hill--if it is worth
while, for the momentary gratification of vanity, to open a door which
will let in complete disillusion for the life which you have undertaken
to live. Because all forbidden excitements are like drugs--they have to
be taken in stronger and stronger doses to produce their effect, until
the patient is a wretched maniac or dies under the strain. Suggestion
and a strong will are such great helps to happiness. Suggest to your
subconscious mind that you are perfectly happy and contented with your
legitimate mate--make the current between you one of tenderness and
charm, and sternly control every unbalanced fancy.

I quote here another of my maxims: "It is a wise man who knows when he
is happy and can appreciate the divine bliss of the tangible _now_.
Most of us retrospect or anticipate, and so lose the present."

Do not retrospect--do not anticipate. Go on from day to day enjoying
the good things which fate has given you: manage them like a careful
housewife--use forethought--quite a different thing to anticipation!
Recognize that you are happy and decide what makes you so, and how
you can continue to employ the methods to keep this joyous state. Be
perfectly calm, and believe that nothing can alter or interrupt the
enchanting present. For do not forget--each one draws to himself or
herself what his or her thoughts dwell upon. Those who lay up for a
rainy day attract the rainy day as surely as those who always believe
that good will secure good. A very useful thing for you to do is to
look around at all your young married friends, and see what niches they
have carved for themselves in the world--which ones are considered
and have prestige, which are treated as nobodies, which are laughed
at or pitied. Then try to decide upon the grade in public opinion
you would desire to occupy yourself, and what are the causes of your
friends being in whatever places they are. You will get a number of
advantageous hints if you do this before embarking upon marriage
yourself.

You will find that simplicity, good manners, and absence of all
pretense are things which attract everyone. You will be wise never to
be drawn into a set one iota lower than the one you wish to shine in.
Weed your acquaintances and remain faithful to your friends. Society
is composed, so to speak, of three loops. There is a very common loop,
which, at its upper edge, slightly overlaps the one above it, so that
the best of these common people will just be seen at the worst of the
middle loop's parties. The middle loop, in its turn, overlaps at its
highest point the third and great loop, which never mingles with the
first and lowest one. Enter society by the best door available, and
see that you are not drawn to the lower edge of your loop, and so into
the vortex beneath. A large section of the world rave and storm that
people are snobs who desire to be in the best society, but they forget
that it is entirely the most amusing, the most intelligent, and the
most desirable, and therefore a very natural goal for newcomers to
aim at. The cleverest men go where they meet the cleverest and most
entertaining women. And these are naturally to be found among the
leisured classes, who have had time to polish all their attractions,
who have had money enough to see the world and cultivate their critical
faculties, who have learned to dress and to move and to please the eye
and ear, and whose abodes provide their guests not only with rich food
and drink and spacious rooms, but surround them with an atmosphere
of taste and distinction as well. And when you see people with great
riches commanding no prestige, you may know it is because in themselves
they have failed to come up to the standard of what the best society
requires. It is also the fashion to say wealth is necessary to a
position in society. It may be, if you are only trying to enter it, but
it is certainly not the case if you have a right to your position, and
are already there. Then, if you have just a sufficiency to swim with
the tide, and are charming and agreeable in yourself, you can create a
position for yourself and be the desired guest at all the best houses.

Men are not angels, but spoilt, attractive darlings! And very few come
anywhere near the heroes of romance. If you fall in love with one who
may in any way be unequal to yourself--in family, in position, in
education, or in wealth--do not, when you are married, ever under any
circumstances taunt him with the fact. Never insinuate or infer that
because of ANY superiority, you are mistress of the situation. The man,
although he may forgive you, will never recover from the sting and the
humiliation, and you will have created a canker in his feelings for
you which nothing you can ever afterward do will heal. Remember that,
if you have married a man in anything inferior to yourself, you did
it deliberately and because you were convinced at the time that what
he had to offer you in exchange was worth while accepting. In these
days no one is forced into marriage. And nothing can be meaner or
more unladylike than to insinuate even to your husband that you are
superior. Where love is, there never should be any desire to humiliate,
and, when love flies away, friendship can stay, and dignity and respect
take his place.

If your husband has a fine spirit you will have wounded him beyond
redress by taunting him, and, if he has a small mind, you will have
galled him into enmity, besides having fallen far short of that respect
for yourself which is the mainstay of my basic principle.

A woman recently wrote to me in great indignation. She said it would
seem that my sympathy was entirely with men; they were all angels,
and it was always the woman's fault if trouble came, and my general
teaching was that women should pander to men and be subservient! Of
course this is all nonsense.

Sometimes it is the man's fault and sometimes the woman's. In England,
where there are not enough men to go around, it is the women who often
have to be the hunters--and therefore their methods have to be quite
different from those of the Americans in whose country there are plenty
of mates, and women can pick and chose. But in every country it is
the female brain which has to do the thinking how to keep the male
attracted to her, when once wedded to her, because of the fundamental
roving nature-instinct which the Creator of the scheme of things
thought fit to implant in man to insure the species being carried on
against all the tremendous odds surrounding life on this planet.

It is a natural instinct for a man to be unfaithful--and it is only
the efforts of social and religious training since the Christian era
began which in any degree keep this natural tendency in check. For a
man to have twenty wives only aids replenishment of humanity--therefore
Nature, who is a ruthless dame where her laws are concerned, would
encourage him, even if the law does not allow him to have twenty, at
least to have the subconscious desire for any new beauty he may see.
So if the woman who is his wife by law does not fill his imagination
to the exclusion of vagrant fancies, the man has to fight a natural
instinct to remain faithful to her.

Everything we experience goes back to some subconscious urge. And
the subconscious urge in man is for many mates, so that nature may
be satisfied by a prolific crop of the species homo. Thus, even in a
country like America, where mates abound, and women can give themselves
what airs they please before they have selected (not "secured," as in
other countries!) a mate, they have still the same difficulty to hold
his fancy afterwards as the less fortunate English woman. Difficulty to
hold that individual man's fancy, I mean, but no difficulty to acquire
a second husband when the first has been divorced, for they then go
back to their original status, which is governed by the law of demand
and supply.

In England the great difficulty in finding a husband at all makes
the women very careful to try to please the creatures afterwards! In
America, as they have had plenty to choose from, it is not likely
that the point of view should be the same. But once the wedding ring
is on, the hunt is over; and then the man is once more under the
influence of the inexorable nature instinct. He is faithful by law, and
by tradition; he keeps his desires under by hard work and principle;
but underneath, if his wife has grown to bore him, his spirit, at all
events, desires change.

These are the hard facts--and women can disguise them to themselves
as they will, they still remain. And with women, if the man is a cold
and matter-of-fact lover, the nature instinct will unconsciously be
unsatisfied, and will be at the mercy of some new illuminator who
may pass by and suggest to the woman's subconscious mind, without a
single word or inference to the conscious one, that he might be a more
agreeable and satisfactory mate.

All this question of fidelity and infidelity is a matter of satisfied
or unsatisfied nature instinct. And the higher the advancement of the
spirit the less men and women are swayed by the nature instinct, and
the more they can appreciate the qualities of mind which alone hold
when youth, and the desire for physical reproduction, are waning. That
is why I am such an advocate of romance, because it draws the physical
into the realms of the spirit, and is an aid to fidelity.

The finer the spirit, the more it craves for a mate for the soul, and
the less importance it attaches to the body. Marriages, entered into
because of the thrill of the flesh in propinquity, have not much chance
of lasting happiness ahead because the thrill nerves grow numb with
over-stimulation, just as the sight nerves grow dim with over-strain.
I feel so sad when I go to weddings as a rule, especially in countries
where nine-tenths of them are constructed because the parties believe
that they are in love and that it is going to last forever. It is like
watching raw recruits with no training plunging into the front line of
a battle. And it could all be so different if fundamental reasons for
things were more clearly understood. People are always talking about
the mystery of human nature--there is little mystery if only it is
reasonably analyzed down to primitive instincts; and the effect of the
spirit upon it as a factor is fairly gauged.

I have received many letters from worried wives asking how they can
keep their husbands home in the evenings. The main object and desire
of these women seems the invention of some method that will keep men
at home regardless of their own feelings on the score. Women crave
attention, affection, companionship, and admiration. If the husband is
not at home to satisfy this craving, then the wife is miserable and
unhappy--there is a void, a great emptiness.

The wife's feelings are warm and affectionate; she sees no reason why
her husband's should not be just as warm and responsive. "What has she
done?" she asks herself. She is kind, looks after his bodily needs,
and in general performs all the wifely duties expected and demanded.
Why, then, does he prefer the society of other men to hers. Why does he
utter foolish and futile excuses in order to get rid of her? In most
cases, it seems to me, the nature of the loving woman is such that she
does not hold herself responsible for the gradual appearance of this
state of affairs. Her pride will not allow her to blame herself.

The truth is that, the more accustomed a man becomes to something, the
more he sees of a possession, the more commonplace it grows. A husband
takes his wife for granted, only if she allows him to.

It is very foolish for a wife to expect her husband to do something
he does not want to do--like staying at home every night. Let her
ask herself what real enjoyment she derives from the picture of him
remaining merely to please her--because he thinks it is the proper
thing to do and will try to make the best of it.

If a husband frequently wants to go out after dinner, do not become
piqued. Do not weep and say he does not love you any more. If the
pleasures he indulges in out of your sight are healthy, ordinary ones
that most men like, do not, by any means, insist that he stay with
you. He most likely will do what he most wants to regardless of how
bewitching you may judge yourself to be. So make the most of it. You
may think: "Here is John giving his time and attention to his club and
his sports and his work when he might be with me, whom he said he loved
so madly and could never, never be without." But did you ever stop to
think that if John wanted to be with you more than he wanted to do
these other things, he would be with you, and that if he is not, it is
because you have lost your power to hold him?

In the days when John was courting you, he was very much like a
leech. He hung around to the point of distraction. He wrote letters,
telephoned, sent flowers, dreamed about you--you were his whole
existence. Then marriage came and the years went on. Perhaps children
later. You were busy all day long attending to John's wants and the
needs of John's children. You were no longer the elusive, doubtful
creature.

Do you not know that this male creature is a fighting, acquisitive
character who wanted you because he had to work hard for you, perhaps
even win you from other armed knights? For a time he was the old-time
sweetheart. You gloried in his caresses and you were positive this
was heaven. No note of alarm entered into your scheme of things. Your
life was so beautiful, interrupted only by an occasional quarrel which
cemented your love the closer--or seemed to. You hardly noticed, at
first, that John's terms of endearment grew gradually less fervid
and frequent. Finally you awoke. John had become so different--so
indifferent--and here you are.

Now you want to know just how to make John desire you more than
anything else. Once more you want him to stay by your side because
he wants you. If you yearn for him to come back again to that happy
romantic state, you have a very, very great amount of subtle work and
scheming on your hands. It will not be easy. John will not be aware
of any change at first. It will be difficult even to get him to think
of you or observe you once more. But go back over those early days of
courtship and ask yourself frankly in just what ways you have changed.
Have you lost your attractiveness to other men? Though a husband may
not be jealous or suspicious of any attentions showered by other men,
he cannot help but be watchful and cautious lest that which he worked
so hard to get might slip from his grasp. You may not be able to bring
John back to the fever pitch of his early love. That would be well-nigh
impossible. It seems that the early mad passion is a divine-sent gift
that takes men far above their ordinary capacity for love, affection,
and the real spirit of romance. I do not say that this exaltation
of the senses and spirit cannot be felt and experienced a second
time--with another woman. But I do not think that it can be brought
back fully in the same two persons after a certain number of years.
Yet John may, all unknowingly, once more look upon you with pronounced
approval. He can be made to divide his spare time with you--to act more
like his old lovable self--the man you married.

Do not hope, however, to get John to the point where he is spending all
his time with you. You would be defeating your own ends, because you
could not scheme always and incessantly to be a super-charming wife and
woman. You will gather your reward for bringing John out of his present
aggravated state of indifference by the added joys you and he will
receive from his renewed attentions. To do this successfully, you must
not allow John to stay at home every night, even if you have to go out
yourself and leave him to his own devices.

Cultivate a few of your friends of younger days, neglected since your
marriage. Let John know that you do not depend entirely on him for
pleasures. Never let him feel that you are unhappy because he is not
about; cleverly give him the impression that you are having as good
times as ever he was able to give. Do not carry your parties and girl
friends too far, however. Do not go out to the neglect of your wifely
duties. Keep your home just as clean and tidy and orderly as ever. Look
after John's needs but let him know you are doing those things because
you are fulfilling your share of the marriage contract, even as he.
Do not let him imagine you are going to any great extremes to please
him and to make him remain with you in the evening, for that would only
flatter his vanity and make him all the more sure of you.

Inasmuch as man is a hunter, his eyes will be attracted to the most
beautiful and the most amiable thing about him. You must strive to be
that creature. You will have other women to compete with. John, should
he ever compare you, must never find you sadly lacking in a meticulous
care of your person, in proper conduct in public--in amiability--in
general cleverness.

Of course, since your husband is your husband, he has the right to
certain relations, which, nevertheless, he should not be allowed to
abuse. With regard to physical contact, never allow John to say or do
things to you that you would brand as coarse and vulgar in other men.
Do not allow him any liberties of speech not in good taste. In other
words, strive always to keep up the illusion that he does not entirely
know you or possess you--that there is a certain mysterious reserve
about you that even yet it might be interesting to probe.

Do not kiss and fondle your husband too much, and by too much I mean
more than a very, very little. To what extent is a woman thrilled to
always be caressing a man who stands or sits like a stolid graven
statue? If your husband feels the need of affection, he will manifest
it. Even then, it is often wise to put him off. Some husbands kiss a
wife out of a sense of duty, or they do it with the air of a general
bestowing a medal on some brave soldier who has followed out orders to
the letter. Be sure the fond touchings your husband bestows upon you
come from the heart and not from habit.

Be elusive--always. Do not be too late in becoming elusive, either.
Begin from the very first day you are married.

Do not boast about the pretty things men have said to you, or gallant
acts they have performed. You might cleverly now and then get over to
your husband very tactfully, as if jesting, the fact that so-and-so
said or did such a thing. Do not do it in such a fashion, however,
that he will think you a vain, coquettish creature who believes all the
pretty compliments men are so prone to pay to interesting women. Do
not believe them yourself. Take all compliments, except those of your
husband, with a grain of salt. Men just cannot help wishing to make
an impression on a desirable one, a trait which has been tremendously
abetted and encouraged by foolish women who would like to believe that
men mean all they say.

It might be wise to start off such a régime of making yourself
elusive by taking your vacation this year away from your husband.
Leave him to his own devices. Let him miss your tender and solicitous
administrations--let him appreciate your worth by your absence. You
might even write him an occasional letter just to advise him that you
are enjoying yourself and that you may extend your playtime another
week or two.

Do not, above all, be needlessly and heedlessly extravagant. If you are
spending nearly all your husband earns, is it unreasonable to suppose
that he will not have the kindliest feelings toward you? When later
years come and he is not so prosperous, as is often the case, he will
have an excellent opportunity to place the blame for his condition
on you. You may answer: "But he spends money right and left for this
and that. He is extravagant, why should not I be?" Two wrongs do not
necessarily make a right. It is your husband's money. If he spends
it lavishly but healthfully, that is his blame and not your right to
do the same. I do not mean that you should deny yourself useful and
pleasant expenditures. You may even indulge in the trifles so dear to
a woman's heart, according to your husband's income. But be sensible.
Do not make John sorry he married you. Do not make him realize he took
on considerably more than he bargained for. Be a help, not a detriment.
Your example will do much toward suppressing any extravagance which
he may have. You easily can control some of the tendencies of an
extravagant husband by your lack of enthusiasm over a contemplated and
expensive pleasure-tour, the purchase of a summer cottage, or a boat,
and the other endless things men love.

On the other hand, do not be penurious. I have noticed that women of
too saving a nature usually do not make sweethearts, the kind of women
men love.

Develop your talent. A man does not like to see an idle, time-wasting
woman. She is too apt to pall upon him, to demand too much of his
attention to entertain her. And she is likely to become gossipy and
discontented. Learn to make your own clothes, if you have the time.
Develop and train your ability to play or sing. Well-rendered music and
songs have lured many a husband to a delightful evening at home. Do not
be a sloth. If you have no children--the tendencies of to-day seem very
much in that direction--take up some art or vocation you enjoy. Let
it interest you, engage you, broaden you. And try to do well whatever
you attempt. Let your husband be proud of you, as he surely will be if
others are.

No wife can expect happiness if she NAGS. If the moment her husband
comes in from business, she begins to tell him of all the domestic
worries--her own interests and criticism of him and his ways, gossip of
female friends, and so on--she will then create in his subconsciousness
a feeling of worry, weariness, and resentment. He will not consciously
feel any of these things, and in the beginning while he is in love, he
would not willingly entertain them for a moment.

But the subconscious mind registers the actual facts, and gradually
they permeate through to the conscious, and John will know he is bored
to death with Ann. And Ann will suddenly awake to the knowledge that
she has lost all hold over John's affections, and that they are only
jogging on together--held by law. This is a ghastly moment for a woman,
and is invariably brought about by her own fault and by things which
she could have prevented.

If Ann nags because she thinks she has certain "rights," she cannot
expect any happiness, any more than a woman could all through the ages.
She had better learn what nagging is, and begin to crush the faintest
tendency she may have for this defect in herself. If she is working as
hard as her husband is, it is only fair that she should expect from
him that same sort of self-control that she is obliged to use. When
both return tired and strained from business, the husband has no more
right to put an extra burden upon her of untidiness, ill-temper, or
selfishness than she has upon him. Both must play fair.

Never make the mistake of threatening to leave if your husband does
not cease some particularly obnoxious behavior. Such threats are
successful, if ever, only because John would detest the publicity, not
because he would be particularly broken-hearted at losing you.

There are so many things I could warn you against doing. There are
endless suggestions for handling a recalcitrant male, but after all,
you will have to manage your situation according to the conditions
that obtain, according to your nature and that of your husband. Be
sure you know him. Be positive you know yourself. Then go ahead. You
may make some mistakes. But you will be reaching toward a wonderful
goal; you will be making your husband happy as well as yourself. And
you will receive your just reward. Dividends may come slowly at first.
But do not falter. After a while you may be delightfully surprised at
yourself--and John.




CHAPTER VI

WHY LOVE OFTEN DIES


I am reminded of a very disturbing aspect of love by a letter I
received from a young girl called Cora, inquiring why it is that she
finds that she no longer feels any thrills with her fiancé, whom
we will call Bob. She adored him only a month ago when they became
engaged. She has seen him every day since in quite uninterrupted bliss,
but she now writes, she has come to the conclusion that there is
"nothing in it" and she no longer loves him.

The aspect which, to me, is disturbing, is the inherent desire in all
human beings for change. Satiation sets in if we have too much of
anything and then all charm departs. Fifty years ago when ordinary
people never thought about any psychological reasonings, pairs rushed
into love. The thing was considered a sort of divine madness. The
cause of its coming was not understood, nor the cause of its going.
It was just accepted as a delicious and mysterious happening and
enjoyed while it lasted, then wept over when it went. The one who
became tired was reproached and considered wicked and fickle and the
one who continued loving was lauded as faithful and worthy! But now we
know that no man or woman can stay feeling in love by will, and that,
therefore, when the partner no longer can cause thrills, the partner is
to blame, not the poor young man or girl--as the case may be--who has
ceased to love.

Bob is to blame--he has bored Cora, who naturally enjoyed the sensation
and emotion of being in love, and therefore would not have thrown it
away if she could have helped herself. Bob was stupid and came to see
her too often. Then nerves of emotion became numb with over-exaltation,
their characters probably were not real mates and there it was!

As Cora began to yawn first, presumably her charm was stronger than
Bob's, since he is still ardent. It must be the case with numbers of
fiancés if they would admit it. The mercy is that in this case it has
occurred before marriage so that they can part in time.

The reason that love comes is because each partner is unconsciously
giving forth magnetic waves which attract the other--and its stay is
entirely dependent upon the strength of these waves. If either loses
power, the other partner ceases to be drawn, and automatically ceases
to feel what we call love. How illogical, then, to reproach either one
for what cannot possibly be his or her fault!--and for that which is,
technically, your own.

Now is there any way in which human intelligence can guard against this
loss of magnetism? Yes, there is. And the first thing to learn is that
human beings have an inherent desire for change, and if the one person
cannot satisfy this desire by having versatility in himself or herself,
the other person will automatically begin to feel indifferent, and
eventually will become subconsciously on the lookout for satisfaction
elsewhere. The most religious, the most honorable, the most naturally
faithful nature is quite as much under the dominion of this natural law
as the most vile.

As I have repeatedly stated, you could not blame a needle for falling
from a magnet if the magnet lost its magnetic power--nor could you
blame the needle for being drawn to another magnet which still
possessed its magnetism, and was placed near the needle. Thus the
only way to look at a case like Cora's is with complete common sense.
Cora must analyze the situation and discover just what actions, want
of actions, habits, tones, ways and looks of Bob's now have ceased to
move her or even cause actual revulsion. Then she can determine if it
is because of satiety. If it is, she must try seeing less of him, and
see if the magnetism returns. On Bob's side if he but knew it--a sudden
showing of indifference would re-kindle the female hunting instinct.

The hunting instinct! That is the spark which keeps all human beings
alive. Every man and woman living is hunting something. It may be
happiness; it may be wealth; it may be artistic success; it may be
health. The most unselfish mother is still hunting something--the
happiness and welfare of her children.

How, then, can people imagine that if this instinct, which is life
itself, is satiated in love, that love can continue, since love between
man and woman is principally concerned with sex magnetism?

There is a deep affection, a love of the spirit, which comes as the
evolution of the sex love, which almost seems to soar beyond this
natural law. But even it, when analyzed, is fed by the hunting instinct
always discovering some new beauty in the spirit. But for the ordinary,
every-day "being in love," which Cora and Bob indulge in, neither can
afford to satiate the hunting instinct in the other by overfamiliarity.

The only people who are perfectly happy as lovers or wedded mates are
those who find satisfaction upon the three planes of body, mind, and
soul--then, when the hunting instinct is satisfied on one plane it
begins to be active upon another, and thus the demand of change is
fulfilled! Suppose Cora and Bob are satisfactory physical mates--but
that Bob's mentality is not equal to Cora's. Whenever Cora finds
this blank in him, she not being in the mood for caresses, will
subconsciously desire someone who can fill it, and be a companion of
the mind. And supposing--worse still--Bob does not understand in the
least any of the spiritual longings Cora has--the breach becomes wider,
because they are only in harmony when both happen to be under the
dominion of physical emotion. Of all the three planes the physical is
the one in which weariness is manifest the soonest.

Or it may be that it is just the other way about; Cora may be an
empty-headed flapper, and Bob may have an intellectual brain. When
Bob is in the mood to want a mental companion Cora is hopelessly
inadequate; he finds her satisfactory when he is in the mood for which
any other woman would do as well! So that there must be many hours in
which they have nothing of interest in common and each is bored with
the other.

It is much wiser to find out what sort of companions you and your
fiancé are going to be, Cora, before taking the vows of marriage.
It is best to do this before you become engaged at all--because the
privileges which go with engagement are so stimulating to the senses,
that your judgment becomes unbalanced and you are unable to form any
just view of the situation.

You may say that if you have to use such a lot of head over the affair
it is not worth bothering about! Well then, plunge in, enjoy your short
spell of bliss, while the emotions are keen--and then do the best you
can with unhappiness if it threatens. Fortunately in America there is
no lifelong bondage to face. Divorce is easy--if you don't mind the
complications it makes if there are children. But when you first grow
attracted by someone (I am speaking now to either Bob or Cora) do begin
immediately to ask yourself: "Would you feel the mind and soul of the
new magnet could hold you in the long, long hours in life when caresses
are impossible?" If you know you would be bored, then have courage to
break away! For the good of the community, it is better that happy
marriages should take place than that the roll of divorced persons
should be swelled.

A good plan is for each fiancé to talk to the other deliberately one
day upon his or her hobby, interest, or chief taste--and see what
reaction occurs. If Bob shows no real interest when Cora is talking,
then she will know they cannot be real companions, and vice versa. If
they like to assume marriage bonds after this knowledge, then they
should be ready to assume the consequences.

I once made the following observation: _It is possible for a woman to
retain the amorous affection of a man for many years--if he only sees
her for the best two hours out of each twenty-four._ If you want to
hold the love of the one you cherish, act upon that suggestion.




CHAPTER VII

LITTLE THINGS THAT TURN MEN AGAINST YOU


Man is a strange creature. He may fall in love with, or be attracted
by, one who is ninety-nine per cent perfection (of course this is
merely an imaginary case; I doubt if any woman would score so high). At
first, all will go well--he will overlook the one per cent of fault,
being blinded by the ninety-nine. But gradually as the brilliancy of
perfection dims, he will become conscious of fault. And every day he
will become more conscious of it until finally he will be so obsessed
with the defect that he will be unable to see at all the brilliancy of
the perfection.

You may doubt this. You may think a man would be a fool to overlook
ninety-nine virtues and let his happiness be ruined by one fault; but
you can believe me when I say that such is often the case. As I told
you, men are strange creatures. The wise woman realizes this and acts
accordingly. Look to your little faults--they often count more than big
virtues.

What reaction do men have to the modern woman's conduct and appearance?
You may think that mere man does not have any decided views on this
subject, but you would be amazed were you to consult great numbers of
them. I have--and I know whereof I speak. And I have received literally
thousands of letters from disillusioned husbands and disappointed
lovers--bankers, artists, doctors, business men, actors, brokers, truck
drivers--old and young--rich and poor. You would be astonished at the
little things you women do that disappoint and disillusionize these
men--in short, turn them against you.

Of course, some object to this and others to that. But there are a vast
array of things women do that all men dislike, and these are the topics
I wish to write of. Bear in mind that I am discussing these things from
the point of view of the girl who wishes to be utterly attractive, or
the wife who wishes to hold her husband's love--if you do not care
about either, then do as you please and disregard the opinions of men.

Let us begin with the things women do to improve their appearance. On
one point with respect to this subject all men are agreed: They do
not care what a woman does to improve herself, provided it actually
_does_ improve her, and provided again that they are not conscious of
her doing it. Take lip-stick, rouge, or powder, for example. Men do
not object to a woman using these things--I am speaking now of the
up-and-doing man of to-day--provided she uses them cleverly so that
they really do make her look sweeter, and not like an Indian on the
warpath. So be careful how you make use of these beautifiers. Here
discretion is the secret of attraction.

Never let a man _see_ you using rouge, lip-stick, or powder. If there
is one thing above all that men object to, it is the use of cosmetics
in public, a bad habit many women are guilty of.

Make secrets of your methods of beautification. Powder in private, one
might say. Use these things subtly, less obviously. Be artistic in
their use. Men are not attracted by whitewashed noses, or by greasy,
overdone lips that would shame the reddest cherry, or at least leave an
unpleasant taste if a boy were allowed a kiss.

These may seem small matters in the game of love, but again I repeat:
It is the little things that count.

I once thought that most American men liked thinness--I still believe
they do object to obesity--they prefer a boyish figure. But if you
carry this idea to the point where it amounts to an emaciation, as so
many women do to-day, you are going to lose in the race of love. Be
careful how you diet! Do not overdo your daily dozen!

Many men judge a woman by her feet and hose--really! And they have very
definite likes and dislikes in this matter. The general idea seems to
be that if a woman's shoes and hose are meticulously neat, she will
otherwise muster, but if they are not--well, she hasn't a chance!

Here are some defects of the lower extremities that will greatly
minimize your chances of attracting men:

Shabby shoes. (A sign, to men, of carelessness.)

Extraordinarily fancy shoes. (Lack of good taste.)

Rundown heels. (More carelessness!)

Feet that bulge out of overtight shoes. (This indicates conceit and
lack of sound judgment.)

Hose that wrinkle. (Would make a very unappetizing wife.)

Openwork lace hose. (Vulgar taste.)

Fat ankles. (A misfortune that should be corrected.)

These are a few evils as they appear to your prospective husband. One
might go on listing them indefinitely, but the above will serve the
purpose for which this is written.

Now perfume. Before and ever since the day Cleopatra's purple-sailed
barge floated down the Nile enveloped in gorgeous odors designed to
appeal to one of man's most highly developed senses, perfume has been
a potent factor in arousing the love instinct. Cleverly used, it is
unquestionably a great asset; used to excess or in poor taste, it is
very much of a liability.

Perfumes have personalities. Some are _chic_, others mysterious; here
is one that is exotic, there is one that is youthful. Some are like
people of good taste and refinement; others are boisterous and vulgar.
So you must use great care in selecting a perfume or you may choose one
that will not harmonize with your personality.

The kind of soap you use is your own business--the brand of powder,
likewise. But the perfume you use is everybody's business, because they
cannot escape its clutches. So use discretion. Do not allow yourself to
be sniffed at disapprovingly.

If you see the one you admire edging away when you get near, or observe
that your husband is growing fractious under the influence of the scent
you use, throw the old bottle away and exercise better judgment when
you select the new one.

Everyone realizes the varied effects of different colors. Red is said
to be disturbing, blue soothing. These facts are recognized by all,
but few perfume users stop to consider that the sense of smell is even
more powerful than that of sight. And the odor that gives you greatest
pleasure may be very distasteful to others.

Since people do have a smell-remembrance of you, it is very important
that you use great care in selecting your perfume. What is more
important, select an odor that suits your personality. An exotic scent
will be ridiculous if you are demure.

In an office, perfume is quite out of place. In any other place too
much of it is objectionable. Be sparing.

American girls, I think, are the most delectable of any in the world.
Even so I want to say this: Nothing appeals more to a man than
immaculate cleanliness. A stunning beauty, who looks even slightly
soiled, will lose out every time to her plain-faced sister so pleasing
to the senses.

Men are repulsed by a woman who tries to be anything she isn't--a
flapper, for instance, if she is getting along in years. Your mind
may stay young through all the years, and really should; but not your
dress, your conversation, or your hair. A grown-up woman who makes use
of "baby talk" is an abomination. You cannot be a flapper all your
life; so the hairdressing that looks "cute" at twenty is unbecoming at
thirty, and ridiculous, to say the least, at forty-five.

Above all, do everything you can to preserve your looks. While a kind
heart and a beautiful spirit are wonderful assets, you can scarcely
expect a husband to become thrilled at your inside beauty alone when
the outside is so neglected. The exterior, somehow, is of great
importance to all men. They miss it when it is gone.

Here are a few little things that greatly lessen a woman's charm in
most men's eyes:

Red hands or arms.

Finger nails too highly polished or shaped like swords.

Fat women with bobbed hair.

Hair that is "doctored" in any way.

Cheap perfumes.

Whiney voices.

Giggling.

Earrings like chandeliers.

Knickers in the city.

This should be enough to start you thinking along the right lines.

Nearly all married women have little faults that greatly annoy their
husbands and eventually dampen their love, if not entirely ruining it.

Gladys perceives that Tom is growing cold. Being a wife--and
an illogical creature!--she will think of many reasons for his
cooling--all of them wrong! The chances are ten to one that she will
never sense the real cause, simply because she will overlook the little
things that make for home discomfort, restlessness, dissatisfaction,
and unhappiness. If Tom exhibits lack of interest in her, she may say
he is drinking too much--wives generally blame someone or something
else, not themselves. Or she may think that perhaps there is "another
woman" and that Tom is a fickle creature. But the real cause probably
is some little home discomfort she is forcing him to endure.

Exhibit No. 1: _The wife who has a place for everything and insists
that everything be always in its place._ Now, no man worth while enjoys
an untidy home--I need not warn against that. And no orderly person
can rightly object to a place for everything. But how uncomfortable a
husband quickly becomes when his wife insists that everything should
always be in its proper place. At first he may feel proud of this trait
in her. But it will not be long before he will feel as though he is
being followed by a vacuum cleaner that is sucking up everything he
lays down. And the whole thing can end in only one state--unhappiness.

Exhibit No. 2: _The wife who, periodically, insists upon rearranging
all of the furniture._ Some women get these fits at nearly regular
intervals. They are a nuisance to themselves and to everyone else. I
once knew a man who told me that when he went home from business at
night he never could be positive what the place might look like. And
if he dared to enter a darkened room, he was apt to get all tangled up
in the newly arranged furniture. Now this is a bad state of affairs
that will make any husband unruly. So I give this advice: In every home
there is a _best_ place for everything. Find those places, put your
furniture in them, and let it stay there.

Exhibit No. 3: _The wife who always tells her husband what to do._
There are a great many wives who adopt the attitude of knowing what is
best for their husbands. They assume that he has no brains to decide
things for himself. A woman of this type always tells her husband when
to put on his heavy underwear, and when to take it off. How many cigars
he should smoke. To eat his food while it is hot, not realizing that
he may prefer it warm. To put on his rubbers when it rains. Don't do
this--do that! Heavens, can't such a woman realize that men, too, have
brains! Or does she imagine that she has married a fool, and must look
after him!

Exhibit No. 4: _The wife who plans too many social activities for her
husband._ Some pestiferous wives insist upon dragging their husbands
from one place to another, night after night, week after week, never
realizing that even a husband might like a little individual freedom
and privacy. First the movies, then bridge, now a concert, and yet a
dance. This type of wife usually thinks her husband needs diversion.
She does not realize that he might get his greatest diversion from a
quiet evening at home, doing absolutely nothing. Or he might enjoy
reading the latest by one of his favorite authors.

Exhibit No. 5: _The wife who expects constant attention from her
husband._ Why any woman should want any more attention than her husband
cares to give, has always been a puzzle to me. Surely anything that is
given unwillingly is not worth the having. But there are numberless
wives who insist upon these things, and what is the result? Simply
that the husband grows more and more to want to give less.

If you expect your marriage to be happy, I warn you to beware of the
following:

Making a husband look small before other women.

Nagging--especially over trifles.

Trying to "boss."

Talking too much.

Interrupting serious conversation with banal interpolations.

Jealousy--when there is no cause for it.

Belittling your husband in public.

Over-praising your husband in public.

Talking too much about yourself.

Recounting stupid little daily happenings when your husband returns
tired from the office.

Lying unnecessarily--about the price of things, for instance.

Giving your husband the impression that all of your friends have a
better time than you do.

Always complaining of some ailment--generally imaginary. If you really
are sick, see a doctor about it, and get well. Don't become one of
those chronic mental invalids, so popular among the married. Nothing
will quicker disgust a man.

Never allowing your husband personal freedom.




CHAPTER VIII

PRESERVING THE ROMANCE AND GLAMOUR OF LOVE


A very good-looking young man came to tea with me one day. He sighed
as he asked, "Where to-day is a man to find a woman full of idealism
and tenderness and passion? They are so crammed with matter-of-fact
materialism they would just laugh in a fellow's face if he began to be
romantic."

I suppose this must be true, since this young man has every charm of
person, position, and knowledge of the world, and must be able to
command the best wherever he goes.

"Fact is," he said, "everything that gave the wearying old
race-continuance business poetic refinement has been ousted by the
spirit of this age."

The sad part about these reflections, if they are true, is that the
modern lovers are losing such a lot of joy--so many thrills--so
much intoxication of the spirit. And I maintain always that they
are the only things which raise the "wearying old race-continuance
business"--as my young friend called it!--above the animals in the
mating season! Glamour! That is it--a mysterious something exalting the
imagination and so engendering, not sensuality, but passion. The one is
disgusting, the other is divine.

The cause of the alteration in the love emotion is, of course,
blatancy--and the cheapening of women by themselves. Whatever they
require of men as a price for their smiles, men accord them. If they
only require a kind of "hail-fellow-well-met"--"jolly old bean"--"good
comrade" attitude--that is what they will receive--so it is up to them.
It is impossible to appreciate what you never had, and as most of them
do not know the bliss of romance, they do not actually miss it; only
they often feel something is wanting, they know not what. For in their
hearts women--and men, too--long for romance.

Walter would like to be gallant and tender to Ann, but he would be
afraid of the ridicule with which he believes she would receive
his demonstrations. Ann, on her side, secretly would luxuriate in
tenderness and sentiment, only she feels that her friends would sneer
at her and that "it is not done" in her circle. So the two wade through
the dreary commonplace engagement time and enter upon the "wearying
old race-continuance business" of matrimony without the aid of any
glamour--and so, as soon as the physical capacity for emotion is
satisfied and satiated, blank boredom sets in. Ann goes very often to
the movies and gets a thrill out of seeing a screen lover; or she goes
to Europe and meets a crafty foreigner who has made the temperament
of women his life's study--then trouble begins. While poor Walter,
grinding for dollars at home, meets some cute flapper who has cunning
enough to act a little romantic for him--and rocks appear ahead! Of
course, thousands of individual pairs can refute these things which I
say, and I am too thankful that they can.

In my travels around Europe a year ago, in a certain capital where
everyone smoked and was extremely up-to-date, I went to the home of
a lady who was an American married to a native count. She was as
different from all the other people as possible. Very, very quiet and
gentle. She never used any slang or asserted woman's independence.

"I don't smoke," she said softly, "because I hate the taste in my mouth
and the smell on my things; but other people, of course, should do as
they please."

Her big blue eyes were tender and her movements were all soothing.
The men were simply intoxicated with love for her. There was a halo
of adoration around her. She was the center of romance. And yet there
was practically nothing physical about her. She had a pale colorless
complexion, and was not the least painted. Although she had a wisp of
a figure, she was the essence of what strong men seem to desire females
to be! And they worshiped her in consequence. For years I have not
seen a woman so surrounded by men's love and devotion. I was very much
interested. Here is Romance!

I wish someone would tell me why the American men--alone among the
civilized white races--should so often be relegated by their female
belongings into the "father class." Continually you have quite young
husbands being called by their wives "Poppa," or "Father," and older
ones, who have reached middle age, seem seldom to escape this domestic
appellation. Now the inference is kindly and protective, but it
destroys every ounce of romance. If Ann thinks of Walter as "Poppa,"
the very reaction upon her subconscious mind would be destructive of
the love emotion and aid the jog-trot commonplace side of the union to
eventuate.

"Poppa!" My blood would boil with indignation if I were the fine young
man I once saw on deck, and heard a plaintive voice calling me while I
was tucking up the dainty toes of a lady fellow-passenger: "Poppa, come
here, do, and fix my rug, too."

I saw no tangible reason for this name, no evidence of children about
the pair. "Poppa" could not have been more than twenty-six or so, and
the wife certainly not more than twenty-three. Why relegate the poor
fellow into a good-as-gold, frumpy, old has-been!

I believe the wives are often called "Mother," too, by their husbands,
but you do not hear it quite so much. Perhaps there is some deep
reason for this strange custom, which may have a sacred meaning for
the wives who slip into the habit. Perhaps it gives them a sense of
satisfaction and of possession to let everyone know that this male
is theirs--"Poppa!"--and so cannot be grabbed by some other woman in
ignorance of his chained domestic state.

"Poppa" would certainly be calculated to put flirtatious Kitty off, if
she heard Ann calling a man that whom she had thought of as "Walter!"
So after all, it may be a wise kind of verbal halter to place around
the wedded husband's neck! But I like romance! And I can't understand
how any woman could feel romantic or thrill towards a "Poppa." She
might want him badly to put eau de cologne on her head when it ached,
pay her bills, see to traveling worries, care for her on rough Atlantic
crossings when she was not feeling her best; but I can't imagine her
getting a weird thrill in the moonlight in Venice by slipping her hand
into his, or feeling a sense of intoxication at his nearness when the
nightingales sing!

But then I am shockingly old-fashioned, and believe this best of all
human emotion should be kept well wrapped in glamour, and not branded
with commonplace. I cannot help smiling when I think how a fascinating
Frenchman would rage and expostulate if his spouse called him "Papa"
before their lady friends! An Englishman would simply stare and make
arrangements to go on a big game shooting expedition! But the best
looking, smartest, most agreeable young American husband just seems
to sit down under it--so I may be wrong altogether and I would so much
appreciate light being given me.

Is it possible to remain in love when you have slipped into calling
Walter "Poppa"? Or does the fact of your unconsciously calling him
that show that you have ceased to experience love thrills, and just
feel affection? Or is it merely a parrot habit, picked up because so
many around you use the word, and meaning nothing at all as a guide to
feeling? But to a looker-on--no matter what it really means--it seems
just a little tiny bit hard on the man!

The re-creative instinct, which is the fundamental subconscious cause
of love, is not, of course, diminishing--or the species would come to
an end. But the form which envelops it changes from one age to another.
Some ages are more spiritual than others, and when the spiritual is in
the ascendant, Romance, and that delicate and beautiful glamour which
exalts the emotions out of just sex gratifications, naturally is more
in evidence. Romance goes hand in hand with religion and belief and
self-sacrifice.

In this particular age we have outgrown the forms of most religions,
although our beings are crying out from some wilderness, more strongly
than ever, for a new faith. It is not easy to feel romantic in the
bustle and hustle of modern life. It is difficult for Ann to realize
her poetic dreams with Walter talking a coarse matter-of-fact slang,
chewing gum or smelling unpleasantly from cigarettes. It is just as
hard for Walter to feel spiritually exalted when Ann has no mystery
for him, and even her red lips and glowing cheeks he sees come quite
frankly out of a vanity box. Neither he nor she realizes it, but
because of this blatancy they have brought "love" down merely to the
thing the animals feel in the mating season. And that is the reason for
the brevity of the state of contentment between them, and one of the
reasons for the constant divorces which now occur before pairs are out
of their twenties.

Love and Romance in these days stay only among natures in tune to them.
The majority must obviously be affected by the spirit of the age, and
this age is materialistic, and it is absurd to pretend that it is not.

However, human beings always subconsciously long for some spiritual
beauty despite the press of business, and even though the example of
others may make their lives barren of it all! That is why Romance on
the stage and screen is so demanded. Self-assertiveness in women and
matter-of-factness in men, kill all romance.

The destroying of mystery between the sexes is another factor for its
extinction. Anything which promotes selfishness is not good for it
either.

But it lies in the hand of every lovely little girl to call it back to
her life if she wants to, and cover herself with a cloak of glamour,
develop a magnetic personality, and retain some mystery. It is a divine
thing to be passionately worshiped--much nicer than just being desired
by one man after another and kissed and mauled in different motor cars.
Try it and see!




CHAPTER IX

OUTWITTING "THE OTHER WOMAN" AND "THE OTHER MAN"


If a husband becomes infatuated with another woman, who is to
blame--the husband, the wife, or "the other woman"? And how can a wife
solve such a love triangle if it threatens?

Perhaps some women may not like my arguments because they may not find
they are soothing to their egotism, but that cannot be helped--they are
the truth--and will certainly help those with common sense.

Let us suppose that you have a partner in some business you have
undertaken together and you suddenly begin to suspect that a third
person is trying to cut in, or that your partner is attracted toward
a new venture which does not contain you as a factor--what would
you do? Would you sit down and cry, and nag at your associate, and
do everything which unbalanced emotion prompted you to express? Or
would you quietly pause and determine what were the causes of the new
situation? Had you been to blame in any way for the occurrences or were
they caused by some outside source?

Then when you had decided upon these points--would you not use your
best wits to eradicate the cause, or nullify the results? Of course,
you would. But in a love triangle the emotion of jealousy so upsets the
intelligence and the judgment that nearly always the wife accelerates
the threatened catastrophe. Let us suppose Bob and Laura have begun
by being very happy lovers--then, neither deliberately employing the
only certain means of remaining so (that is, of keeping themselves
attractive and charming to each other), they have drifted on for a year
or two. Each is really full of affection for the other but the little
observances which keep emotion alive having been quite dropped between
them, each is ready to respond if a new emotional interest comes along.

In this case it is Bob who becomes tempted. A woman appears who has
glamour--she reawakes the thing in him Laura has allowed to sleep. But
Laura senses it immediately. If she is a fool she makes a scene and
reproaches Bob--and that is the beginning of the end--the new woman
appears more precious to him by contrast and because she is being taken
away from him--he hangs on to her more closely than ever.

But if Laura is a bright girl--she begins using her business sense--and
she analyzes what is the other woman's charm? If it is in the very
beginning she has an even chance of getting Bob back completely. She
may notice that the other woman is a more passionate type--and she may
know that she, herself, has grown cold--it may be that. Or she may
discover the other woman can talk of interesting things and Laura may
have grown to bore Bob with domestic worries. It may be that.

Or it may be the other woman is in no way a worthy rival for her--but
she is simply "the other woman." Bob's hunting instinct which she,
herself, has satiated, or let sleep, is awakening again. If this
is the case, she had better be lovely to him--and try in herself to
express the other woman's attribute or intelligence or fascination or
whatever it may be. If she cannot do this--then she had better very
quietly and without any temper, or emotion, tell Bob that she has
noticed that he is being tempted--and that she wants him, while there
is yet time, to review the situation, and where it may lead them all,
and then decide if it is worth while.

She can tell him that she truly understands and that it may be her own
fault--and that after he has thoroughly analyzed the situation, if the
temptation is excessive--won't he go away for a little? Or if this is
not possible, the last resource is to say to him: "Very well, I will
put no obstacles in your way--see her every day if you wish, for a
month, and then when you are tired come back to me." By this action
Laura will have removed half of the other woman's attraction--that of
being forbidden fruit! If Laura can control herself during this month
to be particularly charming to Bob--give him the best food that she
knows he likes, whenever he does come home--make the house nice for
him, and ask in some interesting friends that she knows he always wants
to see, never reproaching him and never looking "pained" but taking the
whole thing as a sort of joke or as she would if Bob were having the
measles--then the natural course of things will be that Bob will feel a
little ridiculous; Laura taking his new "fancy" as of no account, will
detract from the woman's value in his eye, and if he really loves Laura
underneath, he will gradually come back to her.

Only Laura, once you have secured him again, never slip into the
commonplace ways which caused him to leave you. If you want to hold
a man, give him the thing he wants--in moderation, of course--not
what you think he ought to desire, or what you think best for him to
have. If he desires Romance (like poor Babbitt) give him that--if
intelligence, if passion--whatever it is, but only in the measure which
will make him crave for more.

And remember, if he is attracted by another woman, having loved you, it
is because you have failed in some way. So look to yourself before you
blame him. And also remember that Nature made man a polygamous animal,
or the human race would cease to be--so you can only hold his constant
desire by using your intelligence to make yourself always attractive.

You can hold him by law and duty in some cases, but he will be bored to
death. You can hold him by fear of scenes--and he will hate you. But
you can only hold him in joy and bliss if you continue to make yourself
the _thing he desires._

Now what is a man to do if a love triangle threatens him? I am
presuming it is the first time since marriage such a thing has
occurred. Part of his action must be the same as I have suggested to
Laura--but not all--because the psychological working of the female
mind is different to that of the male. Different things have different
influences upon her.

We will suppose that Bob suddenly becomes aware that another man is on
the scene. Until then he has not found Laura taking interest in anyone
but himself--or he may have seen her respond gaily to admiration just
because her vanity was flattered, nothing more. But this time he can
sense that something deeper than this is drawing her. This arouses his
jealousy--he becomes furious--he would like to destroy his rival and
call Laura fickle and wicked and anything else he can think of!

Not one man out of a hundred would stop to ask himself how he was to
blame in the matter! What he had done, or left undone, which had dulled
Laura's affection for him--whether or no the new man was obviously more
interesting and attractive than himself, and, therefore, more likely
to please women. Whether he had thrown Laura into temptation, or any
reasoning whatever.

Most men simply rave--and that is the end of it--and of course the new
interest increases by contrast. Or, if they do not do this, they may be
easy-going pusillanimous creatures, never masters in their own homes
or in any woman's heart. But as I am going to give you advice. Bob, pay
attention! You have just become aware that Laura has for another that
light in her eyes which you once saw turned to you. Then you must begin
this analysis of yourself to discover where you have failed.

Have you become careless in your person? Have you disgusted her? Have
you killed all romance? Have you been selfish? Have you been ungenerous
about money? Have you been drinking too much? What in common sense have
you done? Because love only dies because the other person no longer
puts out the magnetism which arouses it, so obviously you have lost the
power which once held Laura. So you are most probably to blame unless
she has a wanton character, and in that case you ought not to have
married her--and now should be glad to be rid of her!

If, after careful analysis, you have discovered what you did, or did
not do, which left her at the mercy of a new emotion--then seek to
repair your fault. Be lovely to her, show mastery, let her see you
value her, and then speak straight out. Tell her it is paining you. She
will then probably let you know why she no longer loves you, and you
can correct your past mistakes of conduct. Then tell her that you do
not mean to put up with playing second fiddle. Show that you are not
a weakling. It is not possible for men to give women the rope, as I
advised Laura, because of woman's different constitution--it would be
too soiling. And the demonstration of the hunting instinct is not the
same in both sexes.

If Bob gave Laura a month to see as much as she wanted of the new man,
she would probably go off with him. So Bob has to show he is master
immediately, not by anger and reproaches, but while being loving and
kind himself, still being firm. Laura will then respect him, and if
she has good sense, she will curb her own feelings, and if the new
attraction has not become too strong she will crush it out. If it has,
then the game is up in any case--and Bob had better decide what he
means to do about it. Divorce--or eat humble pie.

But many women would return to home, happiness, and security only too
gladly if the husband in the case became once more a lover. It is the
thought of being denied all joy in their lives forever more which would
make them cling to the new man who seems to be going to give it to them.




CHAPTER X

BABIES AND BIRTH CONTROL


Is marriage worth while without babies?

Before this question can be answered intelligently, I must explain that
there are three types of men and women:

1. The "lover-type"--those primarily interested in love itself, to whom
children would be merely an accessory, to be had, or not to be had, as
the case might be. Anyone in this class could get along nicely with or
without babies.

2. The "mother-type" and the "father-type"--those primarily interested
in children and the home--to whom all else is of secondary importance.
Not to have children would be a great sorrow to anyone in this class.

3. The "neuter-type"--those primarily interested in careers,
achievements, and things mental--to whom the lover interest or mother
interest would be of little or no consequence.

Now we can proceed to answer the question intelligently.

The first thing to do is to ask yourselves--you, Jack and Jill--to
which class do you belong? What are you really marrying for? Is it
because you have that passionate desire to be near each other which
must be satisfied--and that alone is your true reason? Are you, then,
of the "lover-type"? Or do you feel that each of your minds craves the
sympathy of the other--and that you want to be life companions? Do you
feel that you are enough for each other in every way and will be in the
coming years? Are you of the "neuter-type"? In either case, you could
get along happily without children--if it should be necessary for you
to do so.

Some women are essentially "mother" women. The child matters to them
in truth more than the man. A girl may have this nature and may not be
conscious of it. She may be a tender creature, and very young; and
she may love her sweetheart fondly and believe that HE can satisfy
everything in her. But no matter how kind and loving he continues to be
after he is her husband, she will have a constant longing for a child.
She will be unsatisfied and subconsciously unhappy.

A young man may also think that the girl he loves is enough, but when
fulfillment comes he may know just the same aching void. He is of the
"father" men. Then a marriage without children is a sorrow.

But in these cases philosophy must enter and the pair must decide. Are
they better off together without children than they would have been
unmarried? If they agree that they are better off, then they should
crush their longings and try to be content. But these are tragic cases
because their instincts are aroused for Nature's plan for continued
population of the earth. They have the natural urge of healthy, normal
humanity, and if either girl or man knows that she or he possesses
this urge before marriage, then it is wise to chance poverty and
fulfill God's meaning. But civilization has developed minds which
create thoughts--so to speak--not bodies--and they are content to have
mates who will propagate their brain children. Their thoughts carry
on--so they have their place also in the scheme of things, and we do
not know if they may not be part of the divine idea for the regulation
of population.

There is no doubt that no marriage that is sterile can be so complete
as one where two beings who really love each other have children to
interest them and "carry on." But supposing that material resources
are narrow and the standard of living that both partners have been
accustomed to would not be possible if they married and had to support
a family. Then I do not see why they are to be debarred all happiness
because they cannot secure the greatest. Only they must take extra
pains to be enough for each other. And they ought to be sure that their
means really are too slender--and not merely use this as an excuse to
dodge responsibility and exercise their selfishness.

It is so narrow to lay down rules for all. The great thing to know
is the nature of your mate before you take vows with him--or her.
If future lives together were studied and planned with as much
intelligence as a new business enterprise is, how much happiness it
would save!

If your mate is a "lover woman," that is, she likes you better than
children, it would be quite safe to marry her on small means and
have no offspring. If she is of the "neuter" nature, interested only
in ideas--or if you are of that caliber yourself--it is even more
safe. But if she is of the "mother" type--then it generally will be a
failure because of her longing for children. So, whatever nature your
own is, be sure your partner's is the same--then you can count upon
contentment--but in no other way.

Should the number of children ever be limited?

Again the answer depends upon conditions. If to have an unlimited
number would work hardship to the whole family--a real hardship, I
mean, not an imaginary one--then I should say that the number should
be limited by all means.

It is not the fault of children that they are brought into this
world--they have no voice in the matter. So it is certainly the
paramount duty of parents to see that they have no more children than
they can properly fit for the business of life. What greater sin can
parents commit than bringing many children into the world without deep
thought for their future, without adequate plans for their training,
without sufficient funds for their education? And yet this is happening
every day, in all countries of the world. What is the result? These
poor, half-equipped humans are thrown into the turmoil of life--which
is hard even under the most favorable circumstances--without the proper
tools to do their duty as it should be done. You see the results
everywhere--pitiful failures, hardened criminals, and all manner of
physical, mental, and moral perverts.

You hear a lot of foolish talk from small-minded people about this
question. The important thing is to look at it without bias or
prejudice and find your answer by the use of common sense.

What will happen to the race in general, and our civilization in
particular, if the best women refrain from motherhood or limit their
offspring, and leave this function to the newer citizens who are
constantly coming to this country, or have recently come, from the
peasantry of Europe?

A certain school of modern philosophers argue that the downfall of
all civilizations is caused by the coldest, purest, and best women
refraining from motherhood and leaving the carrying on of the race
to the ardent and physically complacent, who then produce offspring
without great nervous energy or strength of character.

The array of facts--historical and others--which these philosophers
bring forward to support their theory is perfectly staggering. I would
love to hear an argument between one of them and the ablest member
of some of the women's clubs where they hold the belief that women
are the superior beings and their emancipation is a proof of the
regeneration of the human race. I hold no brief for either side--but I
would like to hear the arguments between them.

Supposing, as a hypothesis, we pretend that we accept the proof of the
array of facts showing that all nations improved when no selection of
mate was permitted to women, who were just forced to marry any man whom
their parents selected, and when monogamous, indissoluble marriage held
their lives--what would have to be done about it in this generation,
where the very idea is ludicrous?

It seems to me that the same result could be obtained by elevation
of the spirit--so that duty and patriotism and self-respect governed
woman's existences a great deal more than mere desire. If only the
women with intelligence, freedom of thought, strength of character,
and talent had children, a race of mental giants would follow. But
unfortunately very clever women each year grow less inclined to
make ties, and the population is carried on through the willing, the
brainless, and the mediocre, with the result that the quality of
intelligence is bound to decrease.

The mothers of great men were generally exceptional women--either cold,
severe, highly gifted, or with a great devotion to duty. I cannot
remember a single one with the character and tastes of the modern
flapper. The mother matters far more than the father to the child's
intelligence, it would seem. I wish someone would write a volume upon
the characters and habits of the mothers of great men through history.
It would serve as a lesson to us.

But what I am wondering about is this: Supposing it could be proved
beyond a shadow of doubt that the views of the philosophers I have
alluded to are correct, would it affect any number of modern young
women? Or is the civilized human race becoming so unpatriotic and
casual that only complete indifference to the future status of the
generations to come would be shown? Put the question to your friends.
You will receive some illuminating answers!

I believe that if every English-speaking woman was fully aware that
the future of her country might lay in the ability or non-ability of
herself and her sister women to produce offspring highly developed
mentally, there must be many thousands of them who are now indifferent
about the matter who would then consider it a sacred duty to have
children.

It must never be forgotten that no matter how fine women's individual
minds are, and no matter how they use them with the avowed intention of
raising humanity, their usefulness dies with them if they have given
no citizens to carry on with like brains. So when all the marvelously
clever, mateless women which this century and the war and the scarcity
of men have produced, are dead, what will they have accomplished? You
may answer, the beginning of a new era for women in the future--of
freedom and independence. Granted--for one generation more--but if that
spirit of independence goes on augmenting, less women of brains will
marry, in each succeeding generation, until there will be no more high
mentalities born in either sex. And what then? That is the point I want
you to consider.

Do you care, or are you quite indifferent to the future of your race?
If quite indifferent, be as selfish as you want to be--eat, drink,
and be merry, for the body of you will certainly die and since your
indifference proves that you are not interested in the spirit, it will
not matter. But if you do care, then it is your duty to study this
matter and when you have come to a conclusion, act accordingly--and,
above all, aid in spreading the knowledge to others.

Far be it from me to give the impression that women should not go
forward, and should not have freedom and emancipation. The higher their
brains rise the better for the race, if they become mothers. The
only point I maintain is that if, after studying the question, they
are convinced that the future does lie in their hands, they should
then cultivate a spirit of patriotism strong enough to make them do
voluntarily what in past generations they were forced to do against
their wills when they represented mere property of a father to exploit
as he thought best. This will be the millennium.

I could never accept for one moment, however, the idea which some
philosophers try to prove; namely, that the warring resentment of cold,
intellectual women forced into motherhood produced the finest nervous
organization and brain capacity of the offspring. Surely consenting and
intelligent motherhood would be higher still. And that seems to me the
answer to the whole question.

Is marriage happier with children--or without them?

Some people argue that children bring the husband and wife closer
together and add depth to their love; others claim that children are
often the beginning of unhappiness because they tend to separate the
mother and father. She has new interests and shares her love between
her husband and her children, whereas formerly the husband was the
recipient of it all. Furthermore, motherhood, with its attendant
duties, is said often to cause unhappiness because of its trying
nature, but more especially because the wife cannot always join her
husband in the old joys they always shared together--so the old
pal-ship is lost.

I suppose there are two sides to every argument, but I believe
the opinion of the vast majority is that no marriage is complete
without babies. A childless marriage is contrary to the will of
the Creator--else why did He endow humans with the capacity for
reproduction?

The rôle of mother is not an easy one. On the contrary, it generally
is a great sacrifice, for which there is little material reward, but
much of the spiritual and mental. The mother does not have an easy
life, nor a very happy one. Although we are told that two--or three, or
five--can live as cheaply as one, it is not so. Consequently, mothers
do without. Housework may bring satisfaction at times--but it is often
very monotonous! Having children is not the pleasantest business in
the world. In fact, a mother generally is consumed by her children.
To her, they are herself--but to themselves they are complete. The
mother sacrifices, gives her all, year after year--actually gives
herself--then suddenly, almost before she realizes, the children are
grown up--they have gone! They have their own interests which are not
hers--they have their own lives to lead, and their own happiness to
look after, as it should be--so they go their own way, often forgetting
the mother and her sacrifices. She is left alone--very much alone.

By this time she has lost her outside interests. She has become
detached from the affairs of the world, she was so busy. Of course she
_meant_ to keep up with the times, but few do who devote their lives
to families. So, there isn't much left for her.

Not a very pretty picture, you will say.

And yet, a mother is repaid over and over for every sacrifice she
makes--in the joy she derives from her children, and even from her
many sacrifices. What is more wonderful, more soul-satisfying, than to
see children slowly growing to manhood and womanhood. The first tooth,
the amazing spectacle of human understanding, the happy school days,
and best of all, the incomparable satisfaction that only is a mother's
when she sees the babies she has so carefully nursed, so religiously
trained, and so tirelessly guided in the right, take their honored
parts in life's performance--for which SHE has so completely fitted
them! Surely this is worth more than any sacrifice, however great.

This problem of children, then, simmers down to this:

A marriage without babies may be, and often is, entirely worth while,
provided both parties are of the lover-type or the neuter-type, and
provided neither are of the mother or father type. If either are of the
latter type, then a marriage without babies will be a sorrow, for one
at least.

If the material resources of the parties are narrow, and the standard
of living that both have been accustomed to could not be maintained if
there were children, then I think they might be justified in marrying
and enjoying a childless marriage--provided, of course, that neither
are of a passionately child-loving nature. I do not think that such
people should be denied the happiness of marriage simply because they
cannot fulfill its highest mission.

As to whether marriage is happier with children, or without them, I
think it depends entirely upon the natures of the parties, as stated
above. You must carefully weigh the facts in your own case and then
decide for yourself, according to the rules I have set down. It
certainly is true, however, that a marriage without babies is not
fulfilling the mission for which it was intended--and the parties to
it are missing the greatest satisfaction in this world--so choose
carefully!




CHAPTER XI

AFTER THE HONEYMOON


It is very important to realize that it is dangerous to leave things to
chance once the marriage is accomplished. The attitude of numbers of
women is that they would prefer to go on being happy with their first
choice of a husband rather than think, "Oh, what does it matter? I may
as well have matrimonial experience and get out of it at the end of a
year or two, if the union does not work."

To begin being happy and to continue so, is the wise thing. No one
should marry without due thought and study of character. And then when
once married--say after the first three months--I would recommend
a stock-taking, so to speak. Is everything going even better than
expected? Is Walter being exactly what Ann wanted him to be? Is he
slackening off or is he becoming more eager? And to all these questions
ascertain--"if so, why?"

Of course, if Ann is a very dainty creature, as nearly every American
girl is, it is quite probable that Walter has begun actually to
disillusion her, more than she has disillusioned him. Walter may be
growing a trifle careless about small, intimate things which he used to
be particular about in the very beginning--or he may have become the
least bit brotherly and affectionate instead of lover-like and filled
with ardor. If so--beware, Walter! For your delicately-exquisite Ann,
with her well-groomed person and her attention to the beauty parlor
mandates, will automatically lose interest in you if you do not keep up
to the mark. She won't know why--but you will not be able to thrill her
any more--just that!

I should say that in England the men are sooner disillusioned than the
women--in matrimony; and in America the women sooner begin to cool off
than the men. And for the same reason in both countries. Taken as a
mass, the American women are the most well-groomed, personally-refined
women in the world--and the same can be said of the English men. So the
English woman who is not naturally so dainty as the American may put
off her superlative mate--and the American young man who is a little
uncouth through beginning work so young, though he may be full of noble
qualities, may affront the delicate refinement and elegance of his
lovely little lady.

I saw two very interesting examples of honeymoon couples last week. At
one of the very chic night clubs there was a young Englishman and his
bride. I knew them both--of great families. They were still in love,
one could see, and he was saying nice little things to her with a look
in his eyes.

At another table three young men were sitting, and one of them perhaps
had been an old admirer of the bride's. He gazed at her with frank
admiration, and the young husband's glance grew fierce--and when the
man came up to ask her to dance the husband immediately said no--though
quite smilingly--she was dancing with him, and he seized her and
took her off onto the dance floor, and as the girl passed a look of
gratification and radiant happiness shone in her face, and I heard her
say, "Oh, Guy, you are deliciously jealous!" And although I could not
hear his answer, by the way he clasped her to start the dance, I am
sure he was saying, "Darling--of course I am--I love you!"

The other pair were American--she simply perfect, fair as a lily,
golden-haired, and just as fresh and appetizing as a May blossom. He
the wee-est trifle "worthy"--that is, good as gold, devoted, but with
no "look in his eye!" The same gay young man came up and asked this
bride to dance--they were all the same world and friends in society.
She hesitated a moment, and the husband said, "Why, of course; go,
honey." And off the lovely little thing went with the gay guardsman.
The husband, far from being jealous, leant back in his chair, lit a
cigarette and watched her with the same kind of admiration that a fond
father would have shown. Then he came over to our table, and in the
most kind voice said, "I am giving Elaine just the best time Europe can
give her. Isn't she a peach?"

I quite agreed with him about that, but with my world-old knowledge
of human nature, I wondered if this unselfishness and complacency
were quite as satisfying and exciting to Elaine as the ardent,
love-selfishness of the other young husband was to Mary--the other
bride! It might just be that Elaine would begin to find more "pep"
in the daring unbrotherly Europeans--especially in Paris, and in her
contrary, feminine heart unconsciously resent this over-trust and
good-nature in her husband--who knows? But in any case there is not a
woman living who does not feel a thrill when the man she loves gives
her to understand that he adores her, wants her only for himself,
and is ready to hold her against the world! She forgives even his
selfishness.

Among a packet of letters I have just received are two so remarkable as
expositions of the human heart that I am going to quote them.

Strangely enough, they are from a pair who three years ago were husband
and wife--both had read my last book, "The Philosophy of Love," and had
been interested in my analysis of things; and both write to me without
the other's knowledge, and from different places, but signing their
names. What an extraordinary coincidence, is it not?

I am going to take the wife's letter first. She says that she was
always an independent girl, and had always had her own way. She admits
that men did not seem to like her as much as her companions!--perhaps,
she ingenuously says, because she had a constant desire to boss them!
She just could not help correcting them when they made blunders.
However, a year before America came into the war she met a man two
years younger than herself who seemed to look up to her as a goddess.
He was handsome and had enough money; her delight to find herself
loved like other women enveloped him with glamour, so that she believed
herself to be really in love, and she married him.

They had one truly happy year, in which she forgot her desire to set
everyone right at every moment and was just human and loving. They had
no child, and then he went off to war. After he had gone she returned
to her clubs and her (to be quite frank!) bossing of humanity! Strong
intellectual interests grew up, and from an uncle she inherited what
in her home town was considered a fair fortune. Her every instinct to
rule and over-rule evidently had an outlet, and by the time her Tom
returned from France she was the chief personage in the place. He had
been slightly wounded and was still rather under the effects of it. His
old dash had gone, so had much of the profits of his business, which he
had had to neglect. In short, the magnetic something which had covered
him with glamour was no longer there.

She honestly admits she had no time for him. Her many clubs, and the
social centers of which she was the head, called for all her hours,
and when he remonstrated with her on her neglect, she felt that he had
no right to criticise her, since it was her money which was paying for
their house and giving them their position! She continues with the
history thus: "Well, things were like that until he went off one day,
and so I divorced him."

Then the letter goes on to reveal that another woman, richer, more
magnetic, has sprung up and has rather ousted her from her leadership.
At the same time an artist has come to live in the town, a foreigner.
He is a pianist. All her friends are "crazy" about him, and she herself
feels his fatal fascination, and that she is on the brink of committing
any folly he may demand of her. Her nerves are all wrong, she has
headaches, she smokes a hundred cigarettes a day, she keeps being
haunted by the memory of Tom and the one happy year she had known.
At thirty-four her life is a wreck. She knows she will run off with
Signor S---- who has a wife in Europe--and what is she to do!!!

This infinitely pathetic picture of the result of unchecked desire for
self-expression and the disregarding of nature's laws, should serve as
a warning to many young women who are growing unconscious egotists.
Now my advice to this poor thing is this: Let her spend a quiet day in
merciless self-analysis until she discovers that every single change in
her career has been her own doing. Let her realize that her megalomania
in the beginning kept men from her, and that the desire to dominate
and express self alienated her from Tom. That it is humanly natural,
and physiologically beneficial, for such a nature as hers to have the
counterbalance of a loving human mate, which she thrust from her. And
that always when nature is thwarted her impulses turn to diseased
desires, which now show themselves in her passion for over-smoking and
the unbalance of her infatuation for the married pianist.

What she should do is to use her will to stop the cigarettes, and her
pride and her common sense to control her perverted sex passion for the
foreigner, and take a holiday in the open air with much exercise, until
she is normal again. Then curb her domineering manners and desires to
rule, and look out for a kindly human mate.

Now I want to discuss the husband's letter I received recently. This
poor "Tom" was just a normal human man who went to the war! His
letter is much shorter than that his wife wrote me, but reveals his
character just as plainly. He says he was worshippingly enamoured of
Sophie--so I shall call her--and her two years seniority seemed to add
to her charm. He thought all her learning wonderful, and her superior
knowledge something to respect. They were so happy for that one perfect
year--then he went to the war.

In France, in the hospital, when he was wounded, he met a girl V. A. D.
nurse, a very gentle creature who made him feel like a hero. She was
not clever like Sophie, but fair and pretty and appealingly sweet. Her
blue eyes looked at him with adoration. Everything chivalrous stirred
in him. He dared not make love to her, and finally he left the hospital
and came back to America, longing to find Sophie and pour out all the
wonderful new emotions, which the gentle nurse had aroused in his
heart, upon her who had been his ideal.

But he found Sophie very much changed--all the part of her nature which
he used to shut his eyes to had come uppermost. She refused all his
caresses. She left him "for a pack of cackling women." She snubbed him
in private and in public, made him feel like a fool; and finally, when
she had turned him into a laughing stock before his men friends, he
just walked off and began a new business in another town.

Then Sophie divorced him, and since then he has been experiencing a
living fight between his dual selves. One side of him wishes to be a
monk and devote his life to abnegation and good works--he even suggests
going to the lepers' island!--and the other desires life and love.

He finds himself intensely moved by the chorus in a musical comedy
which came to his town. He longs to speak with the pretty girls, and
then he thinks of the sweet little nurse, and then of his wife. His
whole days are disturbed. He has to force himself to attend to his
business, which is sedentary. He is losing interest in everything. He
is restless and often wonders if he will blow his brains out and have
done with it.

He fears to remarry lest he should be deceived a second time. His
conscience will not permit him to have any illicit relations. He fears
and yet loves women, and he is utterly miserable.

Now what I am going to tell him is this: First, to arrange his business
so that he may have plenty of hard physical exercise, riding, or
playing golf, or anything which takes him into the open air and gives
him relaxation for his nerves, so that he is so tired that he sleeps
soundly. His thoughts will become much more balanced then. After he is
normal and reasonable, he should look around and seriously think about
selecting a mate. He should avoid the self-assertive types like Sophie,
and the too sugary. He should use his reason and study the girl's
character well before he asks her to marry him, and when he finds one
in whose eyes he sees love for himself, then he should marry and settle
down, and try to remain the master in his own house.

The kind of man Tom reveals himself to be is so often the servant and
not the master of women--and there is something in the female nature
which seems unable to resist the temptation to bully and trample on
a male slave. So Tom should certainly select a partner who is gentle
enough for him to lead, and fond enough for him to lavish his great
fund of affection and tenderness upon. Then he may expect happiness.
For if he goes on as he is, his end will be a lunatic asylum.

I have often thought: What should a modern bride expect in a husband?
All things have so completely changed, even since her mother took on
matrimony, that there is no use in imagining that the domestic relation
is the only one in which observances have remained stationary!

Girls have been accustomed to complete freedom when once they leave
whatever business they are engaged in--and such numbers of girls are
engaged in business now--that I am writing particularly for them.

Well, Ann has, perhaps, lived at home with her parents, or she may
have shared a flat with a girl friend. In any case, she has been able
to arrange her spare time as she pleases. She has not had to consider
others' likes and dislikes once free of the office. So she may have
grown a little selfish, and being thoroughly sophisticated, she knows
how to take care of herself. And knows the ways of men.

So, if she stops to think, without the glamour of love clouding her
judgment, she ought to realize that she cannot expect of the modern man
what her mother expected--and generally got!

He, on his side, has a far more strenuous existence than his father
had. His nervous force must be continuously on the alert, or he
does not succeed in his business. He cannot give time to female
companionship except on Sunday. He is likely to be tired and strained
when he returns home at the end of each week day.

So Ann, after the honeymoon is over, must keep all this in mind. If she
wants him to go on being a lover, she must first think of how she can
refresh and revive him when she comes in from "down-town." She should
have the house dainty--and herself appetizing. Then she should leave
him free for a while to bathe and dress, or rest, before bothering his
tired mind in any way. Then, when he is refreshed, and they can dine,
she will find him charming and ready to please her in whatever is HER
way.

If Ann fulfills her side of the bargain--using her business knowledge
of how to handle men to guide her--she can very fairly expect of
her husband great devotion and much of his spare time. But she must
remember that if he is a real man, worth loving, he will have some
hobby--some interest in sport, or some taste, which may not altogether
interest her. She must not seek to hamper him in the indulgence of this
unduly. For if she makes herself sufficiently attractive to him she
will know that to be with her will REALLY draw him more strongly than
anything else, once the male instinct for sport is satisfied a little.

She can expect in a modern husband that he will be fair and generous
with money, considerate of her comfort in the home, and that he will be
faithful to her--if she fulfills her side. But as life is now a rush,
and most young people constantly spend their evenings at restaurants,
theaters, movies, or in dancing with their friends, Ann cannot expect
that her husband will not talk to, or look at, any other woman but
herself. She will dance and talk with other men, so why should not
Walter have the same privilege? She must expect nothing of him that she
could not give herself.

If both are engaged in business and only meet in their spare hours,
then she will have to keep a guard over herself that she is not fretful
and tiresome when she returns home. For once she lets herself go, and
shows him that she is not the angel he thinks she is, eventually he
will become disillusioned and happiness will fly.

The task of keeping the home happy is far harder for a woman than a man
because women have to be sought after--not the seekers, and the only
way they can ever REALLY hold men is by their charm.

The idea of the old saying that "what is sauce for the goose is sauce
for the gander" is truly a fallacy, because of the entire difference
between the nature of men and the nature of women and the functions in
the world they have to fulfill. I dare say if they were simply to be
eaten by cannibals, the same sauce might make them equally appetizing.
But the meaning which the old adage is meant to convey is nonsense.
And the worst of it is that numbers of women really believe it--and
imagine that men will be exactly the same as themselves!

Ann can expect her husband's protection and sympathy, but not in the
measure in which former generations expected it. And the more women
strive for independence the less tolerance and support men will give
them, so, before they marry, they should make up their minds what they
really want and act accordingly. Many women now prefer perfect freedom
and emancipation to the protection of any man--only, Ann, do not really
want the one thing and then act in the way which will only bring you
the other. In short, the modern girl should expect from a husband the
exact reaction of what she means to give--nor more nor less.

What a young man must expect in a wife is a girl much more independent
and emancipated than his mother was, a girl probably much better
educated, and accustomed to plenty of pleasure. A girl who probably
has not been taught any discipline or been broken in to home duties,
but because of being more intelligent, is likely to learn them more
quickly than his mother would have learned them if she had been equally
ignorant. His wife may be a girl who, unless he has made her love him
so dearly that her true thought is for him and their mutual happiness,
will certainly grow restive under dull conditions of life--and who will
want to be taken out and amused.

A young man has a big task in front of him who marries in these days,
but he must go with the times.

There is one thing about these modern days which makes marriage not so
serious a thing--and that is the easiness of divorce. Because of the
easiness great pains to make the marriage bond a success are not taken,
as formerly, when couples subconsciously knew they would have to stay
with one another forever. But nearly every one hopes it will be all
right, and that a happy old age will see Walter still the husband of
Ann.

And so we get back to what a young man ought to expect in a wife.

He has the right to expect:

That she will order his home nicely for him, if he allows her the money
to do so;

That she will use enough intelligence to give him what he likes to eat;

That she will make herself pleasing to his eye when he comes in after
business, and not worry him to death when he is tired.

He must not expect to wield that authority over her which his father
wielded over his mother--or for that, we ought perhaps to go back
to the grandparents! His Ann will not obey him just because he is a
MAN--she will only obey him if he has that kind of character which is
obeyed by both sexes.

In these days he cannot stand upon any of the rights of a husband--Ann
will snap her fingers at him, and may go to a sympathetic lawyer,
who will turn over the pages of his reference book to find which
state grants divorce for the enormity which Ann tells him Walter has
committed.

And lo and behold! Walter will find himself divorced and having to pay
alimony before he can sneeze! So he had better start in by realizing
that Ann will not stand anything she does not like.

He has to expect to take her out at least part of the time. The old
idea of leaving her moping at home all the time while he went off with
boon companions is quite exploded. If Walter does not amuse Ann, he
cannot expect that she won't find amusement elsewhere.

He can expect that she will be a much more interesting companion than
girls used to be, and if he is sympathetic to her, they may grow
into the best of friends. But he cannot expect that if he is coarse
and uncouth, with unpleasant personal habits, such as many young men
unfortunately have, Ann will go on loving him forever, shutting her
eyes to them, as her mother would have done. Ann will protest, and
rightly, and he will have to change if he wants to keep her.

Young husbands in these days have to watch their steps. "Sympathetic"
juries are all on the woman's side--and no modern girl will willingly
allow herself to be trodden upon.

But one thing Walter may be sure of, if HE is a strong character, if he
has great self-respect, if he makes Ann love him, she will be just as
docile, just as tender, just as helpful as any grandmother was. And she
will probably be twice as intelligent as well.

It is entirely in Walter's hands, as it has always lain in a man's
hands, what sort of a wife he develops his sweetheart into. If he is
a creature the sex will respect, obey, and love--he may be sure his
individual girl will do so, too. But if he is a lazy, self-indulgent
weakling--or an over good-natured good fellow--or a servant to his own
passions in any way--it is not the least use what he EXPECTS, which
just being a MAN gave male creatures the right to expect in past days,
he will not get it. It is in a woman's hands to make a man happy in
his home--but it lies in a man's hands to make himself into a master.
Custom and law won't help him now--so it is up to him!
